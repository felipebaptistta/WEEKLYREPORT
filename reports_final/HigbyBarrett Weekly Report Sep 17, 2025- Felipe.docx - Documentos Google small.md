Macro/Policy – Softer Dollar, Firm Energy, Logistics in Focus

The U.S. Dollar Index eased to 97.01 on September 18 after the Fed’s rate cut, maintaining a supportive
tone for commodities. Crude oil stayed steady near $64–65/bbl, supportive for biofuel margins. Brazil’s
real weakened to R$5.31/USD, encouraging heavier farmer sales, while Argentina’s peso remained
distorted by policy controls. U.S. 10-year Treasury yields eased to 4.04%, trimming carry costs. Freight
remains the headwind: the Baltic Dry Index climbed to 2205, pressuring global exporters even as softer
currency and firm energy support U.S. competitiveness.

Weather

U.S. weather turned decisively harvest-friendly, with warm and mostly dry conditions across the Midwest
accelerating corn and soybean maturity. As of September 14, corn harvest reached 7% (five-year
average) and soybeans 5% (ahead of 3%). The dryness has deepened subsoil deficits in parts of the
western Corn Belt, raising concern over stalk integrity and potential lodging as combines advance. A
patchy frost brushed North Dakota and northern Minnesota, but impacts were limited given most crops
were already at or near maturity.

Central Brazil (Mato Grosso and Goiás) is awaiting consistent pre-monsoon showers; planting start
hinges on late-September rains. Southern Brazil (Rio Grande do Sul) stays on watch for wheat quality
under recent dryness. In Argentina, late-August rains improved northern provinces, but parts of the
Pampas remain marginal as October planting approaches.

Corn Market Deep Dive

WASDE Update: USDA raised 2025/26 U.S. corn production to 16.814 billion bushels on a record 90
million harvested acres and a yield of 186.7 bu/acre, the largest crop since the 1930s. Traders had
expected around 16.5 billion and 186.1 bpa, so the result was a bearish surprise. Ending stocks climbed
to 2.11 billion bushels versus expectations near 2.01 billion, a seven-year high that lifted the stocks-to-
use ratio to 13.1 percent. The farm price was left at $3.90/bu, down from $4.30 last year, underscoring a
burdensome balance sheet. Demand categories were unchanged, so the larger crop translated directly
into higher carryout

Global Balance: World corn production was trimmed 2 MMT to 1,286.6 MMT, while ending stocks fell 1.1
MMT to 281.4 MMT. Smaller crops abroad offset record U.S. output, keeping global supply comfortable but
fractionally tighter. Brazil’s crop was steady at 131 MMT, and exports were kept at 43 MMT, trimming
carryout to 3.4 MMT. EU production edged up to 55.3 MMT, with imports lifted to 23 MMT and exports cut
to 1.8 MMT, confirming Europe’s reliance on outside origins. In China, USDA lowered beginning and ending
stocks by about 1 MMT, signaling continued reliance on state reserves rather than higher imports

Futures & Spreads: Corn futures dipped immediately after the report but recovered to close firmer, with
December ending near $4.30/bu, about ten cents higher on the day. The limited reaction underscored the
balance between record supply and stronger export demand. Wide carries remain a defining feature,
reflecting abundant supply

Funds & positioning: Managed money funds remain heavily net short, keeping rallies capped but leaving
scope for covering if yields fall short of USDA’s 186.7 bpa projection. Analysts noted that futures had already
gained nearly 25¢ into the report, reflecting expectations that supply might already be peaking

Trade flows: USDA raised exports by 100 million bushels to a record 2.975 billion, the only demand
category revised in the September update. Strong early sales and competitive offers were highlighted as
the main offset to burdensome supply

Short-Covering: The latest CFTC Commitments of Traders report for the week ending September 9
showed managed money expanding its net short position to nearly 100,000 contracts, as reductions in long
positions outweighed cuts on the short side. Commercials, including elevators and end-users, slightly
reduced their overall net long while still adding both longs and shorts to layer in coverage ahead of harvest.
Open interest rose by more than 10,000 contracts on September 17, underscoring that forward selling
remains the dominant trend, consistent with storage hedging in the face of record supply.

U.S. Cash Market & Basis:The U.S. corn market is showing the classic early-harvest split between
pressured export channels and steadier inland demand. Country bids softened seasonally as new-crop
bushels moved into the pipeline, while CIF corn held in a tighter band with freight still elevated. Weekly
export inspections reached 56.8 million bushels (1.44 MMT), a strong pace that validates USDA’s record
export forecast, with Mexico and Japan again the leading buyers. Interior ethanol plants and feeders in
the Western Corn Belt maintained firm bids to secure coverage, tempering the broader decline.

International Futures & Trade: Global corn production was cut 2 MMT to 1,286.6 MMT, while stocks
slipped 1.1 MMT to 281.4 MMT. Brazil’s exports was kept at 439 MMT, EU production edged higher with
imports lifted to 23 MMT, and China’s beginning and ending stocks were each trimmed by ~1 MMT.
These changes leave world supply comfortable but emphasize the U.S. as the heaviest source of balance
sheet pressure

So what: The September WASDE confirmed a supply-heavy outlook for corn. Record acreage and a near-
record yield pushed U.S. production and stocks to multi-year highs, locking futures into a carry structure
that rewards storage. The U.S. balance sheet is now defined by abundant supply and weaker prices, with
harvest pressure set to weigh further. Globally, smaller foreign crops only partly offset the U.S. surge,
leaving the U.S. with the heaviest balance sheet pressure. The key risk is whether final harvest results
validate USDA’s yield, or come in lower, which could force a shift in sentiment

Soybean Market Deep Dive

WASDE Update: USDA’s September report trimmed U.S. soybean production to 4.30 billion bushels,
down from August on slightly lower harvested acreage and yield now pegged at 53.5 bu/acre, versus
analyst expectations closer to 4.35 billion and 53.7 bpa. Exports were cut to 1.685 billion bushels, the
lowest in six years (analysts had looked for 1.72 billion), reflecting China’s continued reliance on Brazilian
supplies. Crush was raised to a record 2.555 billion bushels, leaving ending stocks at 300 million bushels
and the stocks-to-use ratio at 12.5 percent, tighter than the ~340 million expected. The season-average
farm price was lowered 10¢ to $10.00/bu, underscoring a more comfortable balance sheet.

Global Balance: USDA’s September WASDE made only marginal changes to the world soybean balance
sheet. Global production was trimmed slightly to 425.9 MMT, with ending stocks down 1.0 MMT to 123.0
MMT. The main driver was Argentina, where crush was raised by 0.6 MMT, tightening ending stocks to
23.9 MMT and reinforcing strong demand for meal and oil. Brazil’s balance sheet was largely steady, with

production and exports unchanged and only a minor upward tweak in carryout to 37.3 MMT. China’s
outlook was also left stable, with imports and ending stocks holding at 21 and 43.4 MMT, respectively.
The global soybean outlook tightened only at the margins, with Argentina’s higher crush the key change.
Ample Brazilian supplies and steady Chinese demand keep the world balance sheet comfortable, but the
shift in Argentina underscores firm product demand that could support meal values even as weak U.S.
exports weigh on beans.

Futures & Spreads: Soybean futures continue to stall under abundant supply and weak export pull. After
multiple failed pushes into the mid-$10.50s, November slid back toward $10.40 support and is hovering
near five-year lows. Ratings context helps explain the tone: late-August conditions were near 68% G/E,
but by mid-September they’ve eased to roughly 63–64%, with harvest ~5% and just getting started in the
South. The curve stays firmly in carry/contango: Nov/Jan holds a 12–13¢ carry (~55–60% of full carry),
and by late August the expiring Sep ’25 sat ~21¢ under Nov, one of the widest end-of-summer carries in
decades—signaling comfortable near-term supply and strong incentives to store rather than lift nearby.

Funds & positioning: Managed money was net short as of Sep 9, in line with the bearish tone. By mid-
August, outright shorts had swelled above 125k contracts (≈59% of open interest on the short side). That
concentration has capped rallies but also loads the spring: a sharper-than-expected U.S. yield cut, a
China buying burst, or policy shifts could force fast short-covering. Absent a catalyst, funds remain
comfortable leaning short into harvest.

Export commitments: U.S. soybean export sales remain well behind last year at this point, with new-crop
bookings concentrated in Mexico and “unknown.” The absence of China remains the defining feature. China
has already covered roughly 95% of its October import needs with South American origin and has recently
paid about a $1/bu premium for Brazilian soybeans over U.S. Gulf values, leaving U.S. exporters sidelined.
USDA’s weekly data showed sales of 19.9 million bushels and inspections of 16.6 million for the week
ending September 18, consistent with sluggish demand compared to Brazil’s aggressive program.

U.S. Cash Market & Basis: Cash soybean markets remain sharply divided between weak export demand
and firm domestic crush. Export corridors stayed heavy — CIF soy near +50 to +55¢ vs November — while
processors paid up to secure beans on still-favorable margins. Brazil’s program continues to siphon
Chinese demand. Weekly inspections were only 16.6 million bushels (0.45 MMT), highlighting sluggish
shipments versus Brazil’s dominant export program.

So what: Soybeans remain stuck in balance: strong crush demand and record meal exports provide
support, but the absence of Chinese buying and Brazil’s dominance cap rallies. Speculative positioning is
heavily skewed short — net short as of September 9, with mid-August levels showing more than 125,000
contracts short (about 59% of open interest). That weight has kept futures subdued near five-year lows, yet
it also sets the stage for volatility. A meaningful surprise — whether weaker-than-expected harvest yields,
a burst of Chinese buying, or policy easing — could trigger sharp short covering and a fast rally. Absent
that, soybeans are likely to chop sideways in the $10.00–10.30 band as commercials carry supply and
processors remain the key demand anchor.

Wheat Deep Dive

WASDE Update: USDA’s September report left U.S. wheat production unchanged at 1.927 billion bushels,
in line with expectations, but raised exports by 25 million to 900 million versus analyst ideas near 875
million, trimming ending stocks to 844 million bushels, tighter than the 860 million projected. The season-
average farm price was lowered 20¢ to $5.10/bu, consistent with a burdensome domestic balance sheet.
Global Balance: USDA also introduced several global adjustments. Russia’s crop was raised 1.5 MMT to
85.0, while Australia’s output climbed 3.5 MMT to 34.5 and EU production rose 1.9 MMT to 140.1. Russian
exports were trimmed to 45.0 MMT, lifting ending stocks by nearly 2 MMT, while EU stocks slipped as

imports were revised down. Globally, production increased to 816.2 MMT and ending stocks rose to 264.1
MMT, up 4 MMT from August, reinforcing an abundant world balance sheet. These changes lifted world
production to 816.2 MMT and raised ending stocks by 4 MMT to 264.1, underscoring a looser global balance
sheet than August.

Futures & Spreads: Wheat futures extended weakness after the report, with December CBOT SRW
slipping to $5.18/bu and Kansas HRW easing to $5.09/bu. Prices have tested fresh multi-year lows near
$5.00; the early-September pop was mostly short-covering and faded quickly. Wide carries persisted, with
the Dec–Mar spread steady near 18¢, about 75 percent of full carry, underscoring strong incentives for
storage. Carry structures were near full commercial levels across all wheat classes, confirming that
abundant supplies are being pushed forward in time rather than rationed nearby.

Trade Flows & Basis: Russian exports remained heavy, with FOB 12.5 percent protein wheat offered
slightly lower at $228–230/MT, maintaining pressure on global values. EU shipments continue to lag,
running more than 40 percent below last year, while Australia’s improved crop outlook adds to global
supply weight. U.S. basis was steadier but lower than earlier suggested: USDA data placed CIF SRW
Gulf bids at +45 to +55¢ vs December, up modestly from the prior week but well below the +100¢ implied
earlier. FOB SRW premiums for 10.5% protein held around +90¢. HRW protein premiums were mixed:
Gulf FOB HRW was unchanged near +110¢, but delivered 14.5 percent HRW into Chicago fell by 20¢ on
September 17, showing uneven demand.

Speculative positioning: Managed money funds remain heavily net short across SRW, HRW, and
spring wheat futures, leaving the market vulnerable to sharp but temporary rallies if export sales surprise
or if geopolitics disrupt Black Sea flows. Chicago net short positions have recently been running above
90,000 contracts, a coiled base that could fuel squeezes but has so far kept rallies capped. Commercial
hedgers extended coverage into modest rallies, further limiting recovery attempts.

So what: The September WASDE reaffirmed wheat’s bearish outlook. Larger global crops in Russia,
Australia, and the EU pushed world supplies higher, reinforcing burdensome stocks and intensifying
competition for U.S. exports. Futures have already pressed to fresh multi-year lows near $5.00, and the
brief early-September bounce was little more than short covering before sellers reasserted control. Wide
carries across all classes continue to reward storage and discourage nearby rallies, keeping the market
range-bound and defensive. Still, the heavy speculative short base — Chicago net short above 90k
contracts — leaves scope for sharp but fleeting squeezes if Russian exports falter, Australian weather
turns unfavorable, or U.S. sales surprise. Until then, wheat remains a buyer’s market anchored by
comfortable global supply.

U.S. Cash Market & Basis

The U.S. corn market is showing the classic early-harvest split between pressured export channels and
steadier inland demand. At the Gulf, CIF bids for #2 Yellow Corn were quoted at +78 to +79¢ over
December futures as of September 16, well below the +90–95¢ implied earlier. Weekly export inspections
reached 56.8 million bushels (1.44 MMT) in the week ending September 18, a strong pace that validates
USDA’s record export forecast, with Mexico and Japan again the leading buyers. Interior basis softened
seasonally as new-crop bushels moved into the pipeline, though ethanol plants and feeders in the
Western Corn Belt maintained firm bids to secure coverage. Nationally, harvest pressure and river freight
constraints continue to weigh on values, but inland users are tempering the decline.

Cash soybean markets remain sharply divided between weak export demand and firm domestic crush.
CIF Gulf bids for #1 Soybeans were quoted at just +50 to +55¢ over November on September 16,
reflecting heavy barge freight and limited Chinese demand. Weekly inspections were only 16.6 million

bushels (0.45 MMT), highlighting sluggish shipments versus Brazil’s dominant export program. In
contrast, processor bids stayed steady-to-strong, underpinned by crush margins above $2.00/bu. The
September WASDE lifted U.S. crush to a record 2.555 billion bushels, reinforced by robust soymeal
exports, which continue to support domestic demand even as exports falter.

Wheat basis reflected the same global pressures weighing on futures. CIF SRW Gulf bids were quoted at
+45 to +55¢ over December as of September 16, modestly higher than the prior week but well below the
+100¢ previously implied. FOB SRW premiums for 10.5% protein held steady near +90¢, while HRW
premiums softened, with delivered 14.5% HRW to Chicago reported down 20¢ on September 17. Weekly
inspections totaled 15.6 million bushels, while export sales were 11.2 million, underscoring how
aggressive Black Sea competition continues to cap U.S. export opportunities. Domestic millers remain
adequately covered, with demand shifting to opportunistic breaks or any tightening in protein supplies.

So what: Basis patterns continue to mirror the broader market structure. Corn shows wide carries and
seasonal harvest pressure, but strong inspections keep Gulf bids supported. Soybeans are the clearest
divergence: exporters struggle with weak Chinese demand and high freight, while processors bid
aggressively to secure coverage. Wheat basis remains two-speed, steady in SRW export channels but
softer in HRW country, with Black Sea competition still setting the ceiling. For merchandisers, corn and
wheat favor storage strategies to capture carry and later basis appreciation, while soybeans tilt toward
prompt movement into processors to monetize strong nearby bids.

International Cash & Trade Flows

Brazil: Brazil’s soybean program remains the centerpiece of global flows. ANEC lifted its September
projection to 7.43 MMT, a sharp increase from last week’s 6.75 MMT and well above last year. That pace
has clogged ports, with beans taking precedence and slowing the movement of corn despite a record
safrinha. Domestic corn values in Mato Grosso hovered near R$60/bag, reflecting oversupply, but exporters
have struggled to clear stocks quickly. This bottleneck has temporarily opened the door for U.S. corn into
some Asian destinations, even as Brazil retains the advantage on soybeans into China.

Argentina: Argentina remains the cheapest nominal origin, with Upriver corn offers holding near $175–
180/MT, but very little is moving. Farmers continue to hoard grain as a hedge against inflation ahead of
October elections, and exporters have been reluctant to register new forward sales under the DJVE system.
Even with favorable weather lifting crop prospects, political and currency risks are keeping Argentina’s
participation in world trade muted.

Black Sea: Russia raised its wheat export duty to $1.67/tonne, which has slowed the pace of sales despite
a record crop. FOB 12.5% wheat values slipped to $228–230/MT, keeping Russian offers the cheapest into
key Middle East and North African tenders. Ukraine’s new 10% levy on oilseed exports has triggered
confusion at customs, with vessels delayed and sales effectively frozen. Together, these measures highlight
how government intervention, not production, is dictating the pace of Black Sea exports.

European Union: Europe remains stuck on the defensive. Wheat exports are running ~40% below last
year, with French offers consistently undercut by Russia. At the same time, drought damage has pulled
EU corn production down to 57–58 MMT, driving import needs up toward 18–21 MMT, the largest in three
years. The bloc has shifted from exporter to buyer, with Brazilian and U.S. corn expected to capture share
once Brazil’s logistical logjam eases.

China: China continues to bypass U.S. origins despite an 80–90¢/bu FOB discount on American
soybeans. August arrivals were about 12.3 MMT, almost entirely Brazilian, while tariffs and negative
crush margins kept U.S. beans unworkable. Sorghum imports from the U.S. collapsed 97% year-on-year,

replaced by Brazilian origin. Yet downstream demand for protein is still firm: Dalian soymeal futures rose
to ¥3,580/MT (~$492/MT) on steady hog consumption, underscoring the divergence between weak raw
bean demand and resilient meal usage.

Other origins: Canada is advancing with wheat and canola harvests, though prairie heat capped canola
yields. Australia’s crop outlook was boosted to 34.5 MMT, though El Niño risks remain in play. South Africa
raised its corn export forecast to 3.5 MMT, adding to regional competition. In India, domestic wheat prices
have surged well above government support levels, prompting speculation about a potential reduction in
the 40% import duty — a policy change that could redraw wheat flows later this year.

So what: This week underscored the disconnect between production and availability. Brazil has more grain
than ever but can’t move it all at once. Russia has wheat to spare but is taxing exports. Ukraine has crops
in storage but customs bottlenecks keep them from moving. The EU suddenly needs corn, while China
won’t touch U.S. supplies. Rising freight costs (BDI at 2,154) add another constraint. For the U.S., the
immediate opening is Europe’s import gap, but the bigger swing factor will be policy decisions — whether
India lowers its wheat duty or China eases its freeze on U.S. farm goods.

Key Fundamentals & Reports (Sep 11 – Sep 17)

USDA WASDE (September): USDA projected U.S. corn production at 16.8B bu on a record yield of
186.7 bu/acre, the largest harvest since 1933. Ending stocks climbed to a seven-year high of 2.11B bu,
with the season-average farm price held steady at $3.90/bu. Soybean production was trimmed to 4.30B
bu on slightly smaller harvested area and yield pegged at 53.5 bu/acre. Exports were cut to a six-year low
of 1.685B bu, while crush was raised to a record 2.555B bu, leaving ending stocks at 300M bu. The
season-average soybean price was lowered 10¢ to $10.00/bu. Wheat exports were raised to 900M bu,
cutting ending stocks to 844M bu, and the farm price was reduced 20¢ to $5.10/bu.

Crop Progress (USDA NASS, week ending Sep 14): U.S. corn condition slipped one point to 67%
good/excellent. Harvest reached 7%, equal to the five-year average, while maturity advanced to 41%.
Soybean condition eased to 63% good/excellent. Harvest stood at 5%, ahead of the five-year average of
3%, with 41% dropping leaves. Weather stayed favorable overall, though dryness in the western Corn
Belt continued to pressure stalk quality.

Export Inspections: For the latest week, corn inspections were reported at 56.8M bu (1.44 MMT),
soybeans at 16.6M bu (0.45 MMT), and wheat at 15.6M bu (0.42 MMT). Corn shipments remained strong,
led by Mexico and Japan, while soybeans lagged with China largely absent. Wheat flows stayed capped
by Black Sea competition.

Export Sales: Weekly export sales data showed 21.3M bu of corn, 19.9M bu of soybeans, and 11.2M bu
of wheat. The soybean figure remained light without major Chinese buying, while corn sales validated
USDA’s raised forecast.

Ethanol Production & Stocks (EIA, week ending Sep 12): Ethanol output averaged 1.055M bpd,
higher than the prior week. Stocks fell to 22.6M barrels. Plants continued to run with positive margins,
though no official implied corn-use figures were reported.

NOPA Crush (August, reported Sep 16): NOPA members processed 189.8M bu, a record for the
month. Soymeal demand was strong, while soyoil stocks came in below expectations, consistent with
steady renewable diesel use.

Flash Sales: USDA confirmed multiple daily sales during the week: 225,000 MT of corn to Mexico (Sep
15), another 106,680 MT of corn to Mexico, 22,000 MT of soybean oil to South Korea, and 123,000 MT of
soybeans to unknown destinations earlier in September.

International Reports:

Brazil – ANEC Exports: ANEC revised its September soybean export forecast higher to 7.43 MMT, up
from 6.75 MMT projected the week prior, reinforcing Brazil’s dominance in China’s nearby coverage.
Heavy soybean loadings have strained port capacity, slowing corn flows despite a record safrinha crop.
FOB Paranaguá soy offers held near $545/MT, about $20/MT below U.S. Gulf values, keeping Brazilian
origin the clear choice for Chinese crushers. Santos corn offers stayed near $205/MT for October
shipment, leaving U.S. Gulf corn largely sidelined.

Argentina – Rosario Board of Trade (BCR): Farmer selling remained muted despite corn FOB Upriver
values near $175/MT, the cheapest globally. A wide gap between the official and parallel peso rates
continues to encourage on-farm hoarding ahead of October elections. Export registrations (DJVE) were
minimal, as exporters wait for policy clarity before committing forward sales.

China – Customs: China imported 12.3 MMT of soybeans in August, almost entirely Brazilian, while corn
arrivals were limited to 0.25 MMT. Domestic crush margins stayed negative, curbing new demand, while
state reserve auctions met nearby needs. The Ministry of Agriculture maintained its 2025 corn output
forecast at 280 MMT, though weather issues in the northeast were flagged as a risk. Dalian soymeal
futures firmed on steady hog demand, underlining resilience in downstream feed use even as soybean
imports slow.

European Union – MARS / IGC: The EU’s crop monitoring service cut maize yields to 7.2 t/ha, pegging
2025 corn output at just 57.6 MMT, the smallest since 2018. The International Grains Council now
projects EU corn imports at 21 MMT for 2025/26, making the bloc the world’s second-largest buyer.
France and other exporters continue to lag into North Africa, as Black Sea origins undercut EU
offers.Global – FAO Food Price Index (Sep 6): The FAO’s August food price index fell again, with the

Weather & Crop Conditions

Crop Progress & Rainfall: Mid-September weather remained broadly harvest-friendly, with warm, dry
conditions accelerating maturity. As of September 14, the corn harvest was 7% complete, equal to the five-
year average, while soybeans were 5% complete, ahead of the 3% average. National crop ratings slipped
by one point to 67% good/excellent for corn and 63% for soybeans. Moisture continued to split east and
west: recent showers in Illinois, Indiana, and Ohio improved topsoil and aided pod fill, while Iowa,
Minnesota, and the Dakotas stayed dry, raising concern over stalk integrity and late-season beans.

Temperatures: Highs held in the upper 70s°F to low 80s°F, with lows mostly in the 50s°F. A patchy frost
brushed North Dakota and northern Minnesota, with some readings dipping into the 30s°F. Damage was
limited and localized, as most crops were already mature or near maturity.

Outlook (6–10 days): NOAA projects continued above-normal warmth and below-normal precipitation
across the Plains and western Corn Belt, favoring rapid harvest progress. A slow-moving system may bring
1–2 inches of rain to parts of the Midwest, briefly slowing harvest but improving soil profiles for winter wheat
establishment along the Mississippi corridor.

South America: Conditions are steadier than previously feared. In Brazil, farmers in Mato Grosso and
Goiás await the onset of consistent pre-monsoon rains, but soil moisture is rated satisfactory to favorable,
and seasonal planting should begin on time if rains arrive in late September. Southern Brazil (Rio Grande
do Sul) remains on watch for wheat quality risks under dry pockets. In Argentina, late-August rains improved
the northern Pampas, but the central and southern Pampas remain dry enough to raise concern as corn
and soybean planting approaches in October.

Global: Europe’s summer drought left EU corn output pegged near 55–57 MMT, the lowest since 2018,
setting up import demand of around 21 MMT in 2025/26. Canada’s Prairies continued to struggle with
drought, curbing canola and wheat yields. Australia’s outlook, by contrast, has improved: USDA raised its
wheat production forecast by 3.5 MMT to 34.5 MMT, and ABARES signaled stronger winter crop potential
after timely rains. India’s monsoon finished below normal (~8% deficit), tightening prospects for rice and
vegetable oils despite a late-season recovery.

So what: U.S. weather no longer presents significant yield risk — harvest is confirming USDA’s outlook,
with attention shifting to harvest pace, stalk integrity, and basis behavior as supplies enter the pipeline.
Internationally, Brazil’s monsoon onset remains the key watch for soybean planting; delays could briefly
extend U.S. competitiveness. Elsewhere, Europe’s corn shortfall is already priced in, while Australia’s
improved wheat outlook and Canada’s drought stress pull global balances in opposite directions.

Synthesis & Outlook

Markets have moved beyond the WASDE print and into harvest reality, where early yield checks and
barge logistics are steering sentiment more than paper values. Corn and soybeans are maturing quickly
under warm, dry weather, and combines are starting to test USDA’s high yield bar. Futures remain range-
bound, but cash tells the sharper story: inland processors keep bids firm while river markets sag under
heavy freight and muted export pull. Wheat stays governed by Black Sea pricing and a swelling global
balance sheet, even as domestic stocks tightened modestly. Macro signals — a softer dollar, steady
crude, and firmer freight costs — leave a mixed but mostly neutral backdrop. The base case remains
range-trade with carries intact; upside requires yield disappointments or a surprise from China, while
downside builds if harvest runs smoothly and Brazil’s planting window opens on schedule.

Base case: Harvest pace quickens and validates USDA’s neutral-to-bearish posture. December corn
consolidates in a $4.10–4.20 range, November soybeans hold around $10.00–10.30, and December
wheat drifts between $5.00–5.20. Wide carries in corn and wheat continue to reward storage hedges,
while strong processor demand keeps soybeans supported despite sluggish exports.

Bullish triggers: A consistent pattern of below-trend field yields — 5–10 bpa lighter for corn or soybeans
slipping under 53 bpa — could push December corn toward $4.40 and November soybeans back to
$10.80–11.00. A burst of Chinese buying, or Mexico extending heavy corn purchases, would reinforce
rallies. For wheat, a sharper slowdown in Russian shipments or weather setbacks in Australia could spark
short covering from the heavily net-short fund base.

Bearish triggers: If favorable harvest weather holds and yields confirm USDA’s high projections, funds
may lean back into shorts. December corn could slip to $3.80–3.90, soybeans test $9.70–9.80, and wheat
retest $4.80. A timely monsoon in Brazil would accelerate soybean planting and cap any U.S. export
window. Rising freight costs or a firmer U.S. dollar would add pressure to basis and spreads.

monsoon onset in central states will dictate soybean planting progress; (4) Black Sea exports — Russian

Ethanol for America Act

The major barrier to a higher Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) is the fuel versus food debate. Now is the
perfect time to move to E15. Corn prices are extremely low, and the latest USDA WASDE reported a record
crop and a record crop expansion.

USDA WASDE forecast corn production at 16.7 billion bushels, with the yield at 188.8 bushels per acre and
ending stocks at 2.1 billion. The season-average corn price dropped 30 cents to $3.90 a bushel. Most
importantly, 202526 crop year is expected to have 1.8 billion more bushels than 2024/25.

With current ethanol production consuming 5.6 billion bushels, a 10 percent increase in consumption would
consume an additional 560 million bushels. The reality is that year around E15 would add approximately
50 million bushels and regulatory streamlining would add another 50 million bushels. At some point over
the next ten years, the additional corn consumption could be 2.8 billion bushels minus lower fuel
consumption resulting from electric vehicles. The ethanol corn consumption increase is likely to be more
of a S curve as new compacity comes online.

With Brazil and the U.S. corn and soybean production increasing, 100 million bushels of ethanol corn
consumption will barely move the needle. The ethanol production capacity expansion that would be three
to four years from now is when the corn consumption impact to the corn market would be felt.
