Macro/Policy – China Tariff Shift in Focus, Dollar Flat, Crude Eases
Markets traded cautiously ahead of China’s Nov 10 rollback of retaliatory tariffs on select U.S. ag
products. Corn and wheat are now exempt from additional duties, while soybeans remain subject to the
13 percent MFN tariff. U.S. officials indicated China may purchase up to 12 MMT of U.S. soybeans by
year-end, with informal targets of 25 MMT for 2026, though no official agreement has been confirmed.
The U.S. Dollar Index held near 100.1, the Brazilian real hovered around 5.40, and crude oil eased to
$60.32/bbl (–1.5 percent w/w), modestly pressuring biofuel margins.

Weather – Harvest Wraps in U.S., Brazil Rains Uneven, Argentina Stable

Corn harvest in the United States reached approximately 83 percent complete, while soybean harvest
advanced to near 91 percent, supported by dry weather across much of the Midwest that allowed for
rapid progress. Recent rainfall in the Ohio and Lower Mississippi Valleys helped raise river levels slightly,
improving barge movement after a constrained October. In Brazil, scattered rains brought some relief to
Mato Grosso and Goiás, allowing soybean planting to resume in areas that had stalled. However, soil
moisture across much of the Central-West remains below normal, and consistent rainfall is still needed to
sustain crop establishment. In Argentina, conditions were mostly dry but remained cool, which helped
preserve early-season soil moisture and supported steady corn planting progress.

Corn Market Deep Dive

Futures & Spreads: December 2025 corn futures closed on the week at $4.34/bu, marking a 2¢ gain
from the prior Wednesday but unchanged from Oct 29. Price action was subdued, with early week
firmness on soybean-led optimism fading into midweek resistance at $4.35. The Dec ’25–Mar ’26 spread
narrowed to 14¢ carry, equivalent to roughly 71 percent of full commercial carry using a six percent
interest rate and $0.05/bu/month storage and insurance assumptions. This was the firmest front spread
since late September and reflects improved nearby demand into the Gulf and ethanol channels. Realized
5-day volatility stayed low near 12 percent annualized, with options skew stable and no sign of directional
conviction. The modest carry compression occurred despite flat futures, signaling basis-led strength
underneath the board.

Funds & Positioning: CFTC Commitment of Traders data remained unavailable this week due to the
federal shutdown. However, CME open interest in December corn was down modestly (3,000 contracts)
week-over-week, with no clear sign of new fund positioning. Market tone suggests light short-covering in
the front month, with some spread roll activity into March. Commercial hedging appeared active in the
deferred curve, but total corn OI was stable, implying a balanced flow between longs and shorts. With no
major trend trigger, funds likely remain net short but cautious ahead of November WASDE and potential
Chinese re-entry post-tariff relief.

U.S. Cash & Basis: CIF Gulf basis climbed to +94¢ over December futures, up 5–7¢ from the prior week,
supported by strong vessel lineups and brisk loadings for Mexico and Latin America. The Mississippi
River system showed improvement in barge flow after rains in the Ohio Valley lifted water levels, easing
freight rates modestly. On the Illinois River, basis edged up to –28¢ under Dec, while the Ohio River held
near –20¢. In the Lower Miss, basis rebounded to –50¢ to –60¢, a 10¢ improvement week-on-week, as
logistics loosened. Interior ethanol plants in Iowa and Nebraska maintained steady bids at +10¢ to +15¢
over, reflecting solid grind margins with ethanol production holding near 1.12 mbpd. National basis was
estimated near –39¢ under, 2¢ firmer than last week based on trade feedback and broker composites.

The interior-to-Gulf spread tightened slightly, reinforcing the demand pull into export and processor
channels.

Export Flows: USDA FGIS reported corn export inspections at 1.6 MMT for the week ending Oct 30, the
highest weekly total this marketing year and more than double year-ago levels. Loadings were
concentrated at the Gulf, with Mexico, Colombia, and South Korea among primary destinations. Despite
stronger flows, U.S. FOB offers near $198/MT ($5.02/bu) remained about 20¢/bu above Brazilian values
into East Asia. CIF strength and barge costs still limit forward Gulf competitiveness, but demand within
the Americas stayed active. USDA export sales reporting remained suspended; private estimates
suggested ~800k–900k MT in new corn bookings, though unconfirmed.

International Trade: Brazilian FOB corn at Santos held around $205/MT ($5.20/bu equivalent), keeping
a slight edge over the U.S. Gulf into Asia. ANEC’s final October corn export total reached approximately
6.19 MMT, up from 5.67 MMT in October 2024. Brazilian exports are beginning to seasonally wind down.
Interior prices in Mato Grosso were flat at BRL 46 per 60kg sack, with farmer selling subdued. Argentina’s
Up-River FOB corn was offered near $180–185/MT, undercutting the U.S. Gulf into some EU and MENA
destinations. Rosario’s corn planting progress reached 50 percent as of Nov 1, ahead of average, with
analysts projecting a record 61 MMT harvest if El Niño weather holds. In China, Dalian corn futures (Jan)
rose modestly to ¥2,141/MT ($6.44/bu equivalent), keeping domestic prices well above import parity. With
China’s retaliatory tariff on U.S. corn set to expire Nov 10, U.S. Gulf corn could regain traction in that
market assuming political follow-through. No confirmed Chinese corn purchases occurred this week, but
traders reported active inquiries.

So What? Corn futures treaded water, but the market’s foundation improved. The Dec/Mar spread firmed
to the tightest level in five weeks, CIF basis rallied, and barge logistics improved, all signs of robust
nearby demand. Export inspections surged and ethanol grind remains solid, yet flat price failed to
respond due to heavy harvest availability and lack of major fund rebalancing. Until either Chinese buying
materializes or WASDE revises demand upward, December corn likely oscillates within a $4.20–$4.40
range. In the meantime, basis and spreads remain the clearest indicators of direction, and both are
currently leaning supportive.

Soybean Market Deep Dive

Futures & Spreads: January 2026 soybeans ended the week around $11.24/bu, up roughly 32¢ or three
percent from October 29, supported by optimism surrounding U.S.–China trade discussions and weather-
driven uncertainty in Brazil. The January–March 2026 spread held a modest 5.5¢ carry, about 30 percent
of full commercial carry assuming six percent annual interest and five cents per bushel monthly storage
and insurance. The curve retained a mild upward slope, with no sign of nearby tightness. Volatility stayed
contained, and trade volume suggested commercial hedging rather than speculative pressure.

Products & Crush: Board crush margins remained comfortably positive as processors continued to
benefit from firm domestic meal demand and steady oil yields. Soybean oil eased in line with weaker
crude prices, while meal prices were steady to slightly higher, keeping gross crush margins above
historical averages and plants running near capacity. Private estimates suggested October crush volumes
likely exceeded last year’s pace, although official data were not released within the window.

Funds & Positioning: CFTC positioning data were still unavailable due to the government shutdown, but
CME open interest rose modestly over the week. The increase appeared tied to roll activity and end-user
hedging rather than new fund buying, consistent with the absence of inversion and the limited export pull
from China. The futures curve reflected a steady nearby market supported mainly by processors rather
than speculative demand.

Cash & Basis: Processor bids in the Midwest and Eastern Corn Belt remained firm between 60 and 75
cents over January futures, underpinned by strong meal offtake. Gulf basis eased slightly to around 80
cents over, as Chinese spot demand stayed quiet. River bids improved modestly where barge drafts
recovered after October rains, while areas farther north remained soft. The national average basis was
close to 70 cents under, little changed week over week, reflecting balanced supply and demand across
regions.

Trade Flows & International Competition: USDA inspections for the week ending October 30 totaled
roughly 0.96 MMT, down from the prior week and about 40 percent below the same period last year.
Brazil continued to dominate China’s nearby purchase program, with exporters shipping an estimated
seven million tonnes of soybeans in October. Brazilian FOB values near 490 dollars per tonne remained
about 10 to 15 dollars cheaper than the U.S. Gulf, maintaining Brazil’s edge into Asian destinations.
Argentina’s export pace was moderate as crushers absorbed most available supply, while China’s private
importers booked several Brazilian cargoes for December and March through mid-year 2026 delivery.

Policy Context: Beijing’s tariff rollback effective November 10 excluded soybeans, which remain subject
to the 13 percent MFN duty. Reports of a potential framework for Chinese purchases of 12 million tonnes
in 2025 and 25 million tonnes in 2026 circulated in trade channels, but no formal agreement or daily sales
confirmations have been released. Market participants viewed these figures as guidance rather than
binding commitments, awaiting official announcements or verified sales to validate the outlook.

So What? t’s still a split tape: crushers are steady buyers and keep the front carry small, but exports
remain Brazil-centric, capping Gulf basis and keeping U.S. futures from breaking out. With Jan/Mar
carrying only a few cents and inspections subdued, futures likely chop in the low-$11s unless Brazil’s
Central-West rains falter again, or the rumored China purchase program converts into confirmed daily
U.S. sales. Until then, basis and spreads do the heavy lifting under the board, and right now they argue
for support, not scarcity.

Wheat Market Deep Dive

Futures & Spreads: Chicago SRW December 2025 futures rose 21¢ on the week, closing near $5.53/bu,
a four percent rise that extended last week’s recovery. Gains were primarily technical, led by fund short-
covering and modest spillover from soybeans and corn. The December–March 2026 spread narrowed
slightly to about 15¢ carry, roughly 68 percent of full carry when assuming a six percent interest rate and
$0.05/bu per month storage and insurance. The structure still rewards storage, though less than earlier in
October as interior cash markets stabilized. KC HRW December rose 11¢ to $5.80/bu, supported by
ongoing dryness in western Kansas and Oklahoma, while Minneapolis HRS December added 7¢ to
$6.22/bu. Realized volatility remained near 20 percent, with speculative positioning still light and
sentiment improving from deeply oversold levels.

Global Supply & Demand: Russian 12.5 percent wheat FOB offers firmed to roughly $240/MT, up $3–5
on the week, reflecting seasonal tax adjustments and a steadier ruble. Despite firmer values, Russia
maintained dominant pricing power into North Africa and the Middle East, securing multiple GASC and
Turkish tenders. French wheat (Rouen) traded near $230/MT but continued to lose share to Black Sea
origins. The European crop outlook remained stable with 69 percent of French wheat rated good to
excellent heading into dormancy. Argentina’s crop estimate held near 16.5–17 MMT, as recent moisture
offset earlier frost damage. In Australia, late-season showers improved harvest prospects slightly in the
east but were too late to reverse prior yield losses in Western Australia. In the United States, HRW
planting was around 91 percent complete, with 52 percent rated good to excellent. However, the southern
Plains stayed mostly dry, and early emergence remained patchy in parts of Kansas and Texas, providing
mild support to HRW spreads and premiums.

Cash & Basis: U.S. FOB SRW Gulf offers hovered near $230/MT ($6.85/bu equivalent), still about $20–
25/MT above Russian competition into North Africa and Southeast Asia. HRW FOB values near $243/MT
reflected freight and protein premiums but remained uncompetitive globally. Gulf export basis eased
slightly, down 1–2¢, amid limited loading programs. Domestic milling basis in the southern Plains was flat
to weaker as mills remained well covered through December, with Oklahoma and Texas bids 2–3¢ lower
on the week. Interior basis in Kansas and Nebraska stayed soft around 45–55¢ under December futures,
consistent with full-carry storage economics and slow downstream demand. The premium for high-protein
HRW over SRW widened slightly as millers favored quality coverage heading into winter.

Trade Flows: USDA inspections for the week ending October 30 reached 350k tonnes, up from 259k the
previous week but still well below typical seasonal volumes. The rebound reflected improved loadings out
of the Gulf, though U.S. export competitiveness remains constrained by price. Russia continued to
dominate global tender business, and Ukraine maintained steady shipments via Danube routes despite
logistical risks. The EU held stable export momentum, while Argentina’s wheat shipments will begin to
enter the market in December. No flash sales were reported from the United States, and USDA’s weekly
export sales report remained delayed by the government shutdown.

Speculative Positioning: CFTC data remained unavailable, but CME open interest in SRW futures was
little changed, consistent with light fund short covering. Commercial hedging interest in deferred months
increased modestly as elevators captured carry. Overall participation stayed thin, with speculative players
largely focused on corn and soybeans. Price action continued to reflect short-term technical recovery
rather than a shift in market fundamentals.

So What? U.S. wheat remains the follower in global trade, benefiting temporarily from short-covering and
weather concerns but still priced out of key tenders. Russian supply continues to anchor global values,
and spreads confirm that the U.S. market remains in a comfortable carry structure with limited nearby
demand. Unless Russian export logistics tighten, Southern Hemisphere harvest issues deepen, or a
weaker dollar improves competitiveness, U.S. wheat is likely to hold within a narrow range. For now,
wheat’s strength is technical, not structural, with fundamentals still shaped by global abundance and
external market cues rather than domestic demand.

U.S. Cash Market & Basis

Corn: River basis improved modestly this week as late-October rainfall lifted water levels along key
stretches of the Mississippi and eased freight costs. Illinois River bids firmed to around 28¢ under
December futures, up 4¢ from the prior week, while the Lower Mississippi strengthened to 50–60¢ under
as barge drafts normalized. Spot freight from Cairo to Memphis declined toward $16–17/ton, down from
roughly $20/ton a week earlier. CIF Gulf basis advanced to 94¢ over, supported by active export loadings
to Mexico and Latin America and improved vessel lineups. Processor bids in Iowa and Nebraska held
steady between even money and 15¢ over, consistent with solid ethanol margins and stable feed
demand. The national average corn basis was near 39¢ under, roughly 2¢ firmer on the week, reflecting a
mild rebound in river markets while interior bids stayed anchored by ample on-farm stocks.

Soybeans: Gulf basis eased slightly, with CIF bids retreating to 83¢ over January futures, down 2–3¢
from last week, as export pace slowed and Chinese buying remained limited. River bids were mixed:
Illinois River locations edged higher to roughly 30¢ under January, while the Lower Mississippi improved
to 70–72¢ under amid better barge movement. Processor bids in the Eastern Corn Belt stayed firm, with
Indiana and Ohio plants maintaining levels between 60 and 75¢ over, supported by strong crush margins
and steady meal offtake. Western processors, particularly in Iowa and Minnesota, were steady near 50¢

over. National average soybean basis was close to 68¢ under, little changed week to week, as firm crush
demand offset weaker export channels.

Wheat: U.S. FOB offers remained broadly uncompetitive against Black Sea origins. HRW 11.5 percent
protein wheat held near $243/MT ($6.90/bu equivalent) and SRW around $230/MT ($6.85/bu), both
steady on the week. Gulf export basis eased by roughly one cent on light demand and limited barge
activity. Milling bids across the Southern Plains remained flat to slightly weaker, with Oklahoma and
Texas down 2–3¢ as mills remained well covered into December. Elevators across Kansas and Nebraska
continued to post nominal interior basis, driven primarily by carry structure rather than nearby
consumption. Domestic demand from millers stayed quiet, reflecting stable flour production and minimal
new spot coverage needs.

So What? Basis patterns stabilized as river logistics improved and export movement resumed more
fluidly through the Gulf. Processors maintained firm bids thanks to healthy margins, while exporters
benefited from easing barge costs but still faced global price competition. The mild firming in corn and
soybean basis suggests demand is keeping pace with post-harvest flow, even as large supplies and
storage incentives keep the market well supplied. Until Chinese demand expands or global freight
differentials narrow, domestic processors will remain the primary driver of nearby cash strength.

International Cash & Trade Flows

Brazil: Brazil maintained its lead in global corn and soybean trade, though October export volumes came
in slightly below early-month expectations. ANEC reported October soybean exports of about 7.0 MMT,
still well above last year but trimmed from initial projections as some late cargoes rolled into November.
Corn shipments totaled roughly 6.19 MMT, down from the prior month but consistent with record second-
crop output. FOB Santos soybean offers averaged $486–$490/MT ($11.15–$11.25/bu), keeping a 10–15
dollar per tonne discount to the U.S. Gulf, while corn FOB values near $205/MT ($5.20/bu) stayed price
leader into Asia. Paranaguá and Santos lineups remained active, with China and Southeast Asia
continuing to dominate destinations. In the interior, Mato Grosso soybean planting advanced to about 47
percent as early rains improved field conditions, although subsoil moisture in central Brazil remains below
normal. Domestic logistics and barge flows stayed fluid, preventing major congestion despite uneven
rainfall across the Center-West.

Argentina: Argentina’s Up-River FOB corn was offered around $180–185/MT, while soybeans and
soymeal were near $430–435/MT, each down slightly from late October. Farmer selling slowed again
following the expiration of temporary tax incentives and ahead of the November 24 runoff election.
Rosario’s crushers maintained steady throughput, supported by stocks accumulated during September’s
“soy dollar” window. Corn planting reached about 50 percent of projected area, well ahead of last year,
with improved soil moisture supporting a record harvest outlook of around 61 MMT if El Niño weather
persists. The peso continued to weaken, with the informal rate near 1,450 per U.S. dollar, sustaining
export incentives despite uncertainty over post-election policy adjustments. Wheat harvest began in
northern provinces under mostly favorable weather, with national production estimates still near 16.5–17
MMT.

China: China’s import behavior remained cautious. U.S. Gulf soybeans were offered at roughly $2.40 per
bushel over January futures, about 15 cents higher than equivalent Brazilian cargoes, keeping Brazil as
the preferred origin for November and December shipments. Chinese crushers booked additional
Brazilian parcels for nearby positions, while reported purchases of U.S. beans were limited and largely
unconfirmed. Dalian corn futures for January traded near ¥2,141/MT ($6.40/bu equivalent), still well

above landed import parity, leaving imported corn uncompetitive without tariff exemptions. The November
10 tariff rollback will remove additional retaliatory duties on U.S. corn, wheat, and sorghum, but soybeans
remain subject to the 13 percent MFN rate. Reports of a framework for future Chinese soybean
purchases, 12 million tonnes for 2025 and 25 million tonnes for 2026, circulated this week, but no official
agreement or daily sales were confirmed. Market participants are waiting for evidence of follow-through
before repricing demand expectations.

EU / Black Sea: Russian 12.5 percent protein wheat FOB offers strengthened further to about $240/MT,
up three to five dollars from last week, as export taxes adjusted higher with currency stabilization. Despite
that, Russian shipments to North Africa and the Middle East remained brisk, keeping global wheat values
anchored. French wheat offers from Rouen were near $230/MT, while export interest was subdued after
recent Algerian and Moroccan tenders. Ukrainian corn exports through the Danube and rail corridors
continued steadily, with competitive offers around $205–210/MT into Mediterranean buyers. The EU’s
internal feed grain balance remained comfortable, and imports slowed seasonally as local harvest
wrapped up. Russian and Ukrainian soymeal continued to make inroads into MENA and Southern Europe
markets at the expense of U.S. product, supported by lower freight and a softer ruble.

Other Origins: No major shifts occurred in Canada or South Africa. Canadian spring wheat exports into
Mexico and Asia continued at a modest pace, while Ukraine’s logistics remained stable under the current
corridor arrangements. Australian wheat harvest began in eastern regions, with quality expected to be
variable after earlier dryness. India remained absent from international grain markets, and no new trade
policy announcements were issued from the region.

So What? Global trade remains led by Russia in wheat and by Brazil in corn and soybeans, while
Argentina’s political calendar introduces short-term uncertainty without shifting fundamentals. China’s
limited engagement with U.S. supply and preference for cheaper Brazilian offers continue to weigh on
Gulf competitiveness. With freight and FOB differentials still wide, U.S. exporters face difficulty regaining
share. Until tariff advantages or logistical costs narrow meaningfully, domestic spreads and basis will
keep favoring storage and deferred ownership over near-term movement.

Key Fundamentals & Reports (Oct 30 – Nov 5)

Government Data & Reporting: The U.S. government shutdown continued through the first week of
November, extending into its 36th day and keeping USDA, CFTC, and other agency data releases
suspended. No official updates were published for export sales, crop progress, or fund positioning. Only
fee-based and independently operated datasets, such as USDA FGIS inspections, EIA ethanol reports,
and international trade figures from ANEC and BCBA, were available. USDA confirmed that regular
reporting schedules, including WASDE and Crop Production, will resume beginning November 14, when
backlogged data will be consolidated. Market participants largely relied on private estimates and export
lineup data for near-term guidance.

Crop Progress (estimated as of Nov 2): Private surveys and trade consensus indicated the U.S. corn
harvest reached roughly 83 percent complete, while soybeans were near 91 percent, both comfortably
ahead of the five-year averages of 68 and 78 percent, respectively. Dry and mild conditions across the
Midwest allowed uninterrupted fieldwork through early November, with most producers in Illinois, Iowa,
and Minnesota finishing harvest. In the central and southern Plains, winter wheat planting reached an
estimated 91 percent completion, with 52 percent of the crop rated good to excellent. Emergence has
been uneven in drier areas of Kansas and Oklahoma, but no major damage or frost losses have been

reported.

Export Inspections (USDA FGIS – week ending Oct 30, published Nov 3): Corn inspections surged to
1.6 MMT, more than doubling last year’s comparable week and marking the strongest weekly volume of
the marketing year. Mexico, Colombia, and South Korea were the primary destinations. Soybean loadings
totaled about 0.96 MMT, down slightly from 1.06 MMT the prior week as Chinese purchases shifted
toward Brazilian origins. Wheat inspections improved to 350k tonnes after last week’s 259k, though U.S.
exports remain constrained by weak global competitiveness. Western Hemisphere demand continues to
underpin corn and soy flows, while wheat trade remains limited to sporadic Latin American and Caribbean
markets.

Export Sales (USDA FAS, not published): Weekly export sales data remained suspended, with the
most recent release still covering the week ending September 18. In the absence of official figures, trade
sources cited continued soybean sales activity to “unknown” destinations, widely assumed to be China,
and scattered corn sales to Mexico and Japan. No major flash sales were reported during the window,
though traders expect pent-up reporting to resume in mid-November.

Ethanol Production & Stocks (EIA – week ending Oct 31 / published Nov 5): Ethanol output
increased sharply to 1.123 million barrels per day, up eight percent on the week and 1.6 percent above
year-ago levels. Stocks rose to roughly 21.3 million barrels, about three percent higher year on year. The
weekly grind implied roughly 107 million bushels of corn use, supporting firm bids at ethanol plants and
steady basis in the Western Corn Belt. Margins remained profitable as corn prices stabilized and co-
product values (DDGS) firmed modestly.

NOPA Crush: No new NOPA data were published within the window, but trade expectations point to
another strong month of domestic processing. Private estimates suggest October crush likely exceeded the
prior year’s pace, sustained by favorable board-crush margins and steady soymeal demand in the Eastern
Corn Belt. Processor bids remained firm, consistent with positive margins despite soft export movement.

CFTC Commitments of Traders: No CFTC reports were released as of November 5. CME open interest
changes indicated limited speculative repositioning: minor short-covering in corn, slightly higher hedging
in soybeans, and little movement in wheat. Trade estimates continued to peg managed money net short
around 45–55k contracts in corn, roughly 90–95k in wheat, and near flat to modestly long in soybeans.
Absent official data, market direction remained anchored by physical flow and spread behavior rather
than fund activity.

So What? U.S. harvest is effectively complete, and physical flow data continue to confirm strong corn and
ethanol demand, steady domestic crush, and slow soybean exports. With official USDA and CFTC
updates still offline, traders are leaning heavily on inspections, private shipment data, and basis signals to
gauge momentum. Corn’s export strength and ethanol grind provided mild fundamental support, while
soybeans stayed rangebound on weak China buying. Wheat remains globally oversupplied and
uncompetitive. Until data releases resume and Brazil’s weather trend clarifies, short-term price direction
will remain driven by export pace, logistics, and domestic processor activity rather than official
government reports.

International Reports:

Brazil – ANEC & Weather Updates: Brazil maintained its dominant role in global grain trade. ANEC’s final
October estimates confirmed soybean exports at roughly 7.0 MMT and corn exports near 6.19 MMT, both

slightly below earlier projections due to delayed loadings in northern ports and vessel scheduling into
November. FOB Santos soybeans averaged between $486 and $490/MT ($11.15–$11.25/bu), while corn
values held steady near $205/MT ($5.20/bu), preserving Brazil’s pricing advantage over the U.S. Gulf into
Asia. Mato Grosso soybean planting advanced to about 47 percent, up sharply from the previous week as
early rains arrived, though cumulative October precipitation remained well below average. Forecast models

China – Trade Talks & Tariff Rollback: Market attention focused on the upcoming November 10

Global – Multilateral Updates: No new multilateral reports were released during the Oct 30–Nov 5

So What? The global trade landscape remains shaped by Brazil’s dominance in soybeans and corn and

Russia’s control of wheat flows. Argentina’s political transition continues to influence near-term farmer

South American supply and no new geopolitical or weather disruptions, global pricing remains anchored
by export competition. The next decisive factor will be Brazil’s November rainfall: consistent precipitation
could reinforce the supply outlook, while renewed dryness would inject fresh weather premium into
soybean and corn markets.

Weather & Crop Conditions

United States
Crop Progress & Rainfall: Harvest advanced quickly across the Corn Belt under mostly dry weather,
with the season now effectively winding down. Private estimates place corn harvest near 83 percent
complete and soybeans around 91 percent, both well ahead of the five-year averages. Dry conditions
across the Midwest and Northern Plains allowed uninterrupted fieldwork, while late-week cooling helped
natural dry-down without frost damage. The southern Plains remained predominantly dry, supporting field
access but leaving winter wheat emergence uneven across Kansas, Oklahoma, and Texas, where topsoil
moisture deficits persist. Despite the dryness, no widespread yield losses have been reported.

River Levels: River levels along the Mississippi system improved slightly following rainfall in the Ohio
Valley, easing barge restrictions on key segments. Draft guidance increased marginally in the Lower
Mississippi, helping reduce barge tariffs to about $16–17 per ton on the Cairo–Memphis route, down from
more than $19 in mid-October. Even with the improvement, water levels remain shallow enough to constrain
full barge capacity, keeping freight rates and transit times above seasonal averages.

Temperatures: Temperatures stayed seasonally mild to warm across most growing regions, ranging from
the upper 60s to mid-70s in the central Corn Belt and low 80s across the southern Plains. The warmth
continued to deplete subsoil moisture reserves, and the lack of significant precipitation left HRW wheat
reliant on light topsoil moisture for early stand establishment.

Outlook (6–10 days): NOAA’s 6–10 day outlook (issued Nov 5) called for mostly dry conditions across
the Midwest and Plains, with the highest rainfall chances confined to the lower Mississippi and
Tennessee River basins. Scattered light showers were projected for the southern Plains, potentially
improving HRW emergence but insufficient to fully restore soil moisture.

South America:
Brazil: In Brazil, scattered showers finally reached parts of Mato Grosso and Goiás after an exceptionally
dry October. Rainfall totals ranged from 15 to 40 millimeters in most central and western zones—well
below normal but enough to restart soybean planting. IMEA reported state planting progress near 47
percent as of Oct 30, about seven points behind last year. Agronomists continued to warn that subsoil
moisture remains thin and consistent rains will be needed through mid-November to prevent early-season
stress. Southern states, including Paraná and Rio Grande do Sul, experienced drier weather after
excessive October rains, with good growing conditions overall. Forecasts show improving precipitation
chances for central Brazil into the second week of November, suggesting modest relief if the pattern
verifies.

Argentina: Argentina saw mostly dry but stable weather, following widespread rain late last month that
boosted corn planting and improved wheat finishing conditions. Soil moisture remains adequate across
key Pampas provinces, with no major stress reported for early corn or wheat. Short-term forecasts call for
localized thunderstorms delivering 20–35 millimeters of rain across Córdoba and Santa Fe during the

coming week, enough to maintain favorable conditions for ongoing corn planting and early soybean
seeding.

Global: In the Black Sea region, Russia and Ukraine reported stable progress on winter wheat
establishment, supported by mild autumn temperatures and adequate soil moisture. The EU’s main
producing regions—France, Germany, and Poland—also enjoyed favorable planting weather, with
uniform emergence and minimal delays. In contrast, Australia’s western wheat belt remained under
persistent drought, with limited rainfall and below-average soil moisture leading to further yield
deterioration as harvest advances. Eastern Australia benefited from scattered showers that improved
late-crop quality but were too late to offset earlier dryness. No significant weather anomalies were
reported across India or South Africa during the week.

So What? Harvest across the United States is effectively complete, with strong field conditions allowing
fast progress and minimal quality loss. River navigation has improved modestly, though freight costs
remain elevated enough to pressure interior basis. Globally, attention remains fixed on Brazil’s Central-
West region, where rainfall through mid-November will determine whether yield risks become material.
Argentina, Russia, and the EU continue to enjoy favorable weather, providing overall stability to global
grain and oilseed supply expectations, while Australia’s dryness remains the primary production risk
outside South America.

Synthesis & Outlook

The first week of November brought limited new data, as the ongoing government shutdown extended to
36 days, keeping USDA and CFTC reporting suspended. Market direction remained driven by physical
signals—basis, spreads, and export inspections—rather than official statistics. U.S. futures held within
narrow ranges, reflecting cautious trade ahead of the expected resumption of reporting later this month.
December corn settled near $4.34/bu, January soybeans around $11.24/bu, and December Chicago
wheat at roughly $5.53/bu. The modest gains in wheat and soybeans reflected weather-related risk in
Brazil and short covering, while corn strength was underpinned by robust ethanol production and
improving export inspections. Spreads remained stable: corn Dec/Mar near 71 percent of full carry, soy
Jan/Mar at roughly 59 percent, and wheat Dec/Mar around 68 percent. The curves continue to confirm a
balanced, carry-structured market with firm commercial ownership but no nearby scarcity.

Speculative participation remained light, with no new CFTC data to confirm positioning. CME open
interest trends suggested limited short-covering in corn and mild commercial hedging in soybeans. Price
action and volume patterns still point to cash-led support rather than speculative conviction. Volatility
remained subdued, and managed money likely maintained a modest net short in corn and wheat and a
small net long in soybeans. With futures muted, physical market tone dominated sentiment: CIF Gulf
basis improved as barge flows normalized, ethanol production rose to 1.123 mbpd, and crush margins
stayed profitable, reinforcing steady domestic consumption.

Globally, Brazil sustained its export advantage across corn and soybeans, despite modest downward
revisions to October volumes. Santos FOB soybeans traded roughly $10–15/MT below U.S. Gulf
equivalents, while corn held near $205/MT, maintaining a $25–30 discount into Asia. Argentina’s political
transition introduced short-term caution but no disruption to trade flows, as crushers continued processing
and exporters retained steady coverage. In China, market focus turned toward the upcoming Nov 10 tariff
rollback, which will lift additional duties on U.S. corn, wheat, and sorghum but not soybeans. Reports of a
potential 12 MMT soybean purchase framework for 2025 and 25 MMT for 2026 circulated through trade
channels but remained unconfirmed. Russian FOB wheat offers rose to about $240/MT after another tax
adjustment, while export pace stayed firm into North Africa and the Middle East, preserving Russia’s role
as the global price anchor.

Bullish triggers: Continued dryness in central Brazil limiting soybean planting progress, confirmation of
Chinese soybean purchases from the U.S., stronger ethanol grind or crush margins, and further recovery
in river levels supporting CIF and Gulf bids.

Bearish triggers: Lack of official USDA data prolonging uncertainty, abundant global supply led by Brazil
and Russia, limited evidence of new Chinese buying, and firm FOB discounts from South America and
the Black Sea constraining U.S. export competitiveness..

In summary: The grain complex remains anchored by physical demand and seasonal storage economics
rather than momentum or speculative activity. Domestic processors and exporters continue to provide a
floor through steady basis and positive margins, while global competition and data gaps limit directional
conviction. Until official U.S. data returns or Brazil’s rainfall pattern breaks decisively, futures are likely to
remain rangebound, supported by cash markets but capped by external supply pressure. The U.S. origin
remains a secondary supplier, waiting for freight normalization, Chinese reengagement, or a South
American weather shock to realign global arbitrage.

Ethanol for America Act

The major barrier to a higher Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) is the fuel versus food debate. Now is the
perfect time to move to E15. Corn prices are extremely low, and the latest USDA WASDE reported a record
crop and a record crop expansion.

USDA WASDE forecast corn production at 16.7 billion bushels, with the yield at 188.8 bushels per acre and
ending stocks at 2.1 billion. The season-average corn price dropped 30 cents to $3.90 a bushel. Most
importantly, 202526 crop year is expected to have 1.8 billion more bushels than 2024/25.

With current ethanol production consuming 5.6 billion bushels, a 10 percent increase in consumption would
consume an additional 560 million bushels. The reality is that year around E15 would add approximately
50 million bushels and regulatory streamlining would add another 50 million bushels. At some point over
the next ten years, the additional corn consumption could be 2.8 billion bushels minus lower fuel
consumption resulting from electric vehicles. The ethanol corn consumption increase is likely to be more
of a S curve as new compacity comes online.

With Brazil and the U.S. corn and soybean production increasing, 100 million bushels of ethanol corn
consumption will barely move the needle. The ethanol production capacity expansion that would be three
to four years from now is when the corn consumption impact to the corn market would be felt.
