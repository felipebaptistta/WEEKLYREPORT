# HigbyBarrett Weekly Commodity Market Report
## Week of March 11, 2026 — Post-WASDE Edition

---

## Executive Summary

**Corn.** The March WASDE handed bears a hollow victory in corn. U.S. ending stocks held at 1.540 billion bushels — in line with the February estimate and 30 million bushels above trade expectations — but the market's first reaction was a 2-cent rally before sellers reasserted. By the close, May corn had surrendered those gains and more, finishing the session down 5 cents. The week's total damage was 11 cents, or 2.4 percent, to $4.42/bu. What drove prices lower was not the domestic balance sheet but the global one: USDA raised world corn ending stocks to 297.4 MMT, 1.9 MMT above trade expectations and 2.4 MMT above February, erasing any narrative of tightening global supply. Funds, already positioned net short 108,400 contracts — the largest bearish bet since November 2025 — had little incentive to cover.

**Soybeans.** The WASDE delivered a genuinely bearish number for soybeans, and the market priced it accordingly. U.S. ending stocks came in at 390 million bushels, 10 million above February's estimate and a full 20 million above what the trade had penciled in. Brazil's production was raised to 127 MMT, adding further weight to an already well-supplied global balance sheet that reached 124.8 MMT — 2.2 MMT above February and 2.8 MMT above the pre-report consensus. November soybeans fell 9 cents and broke below the prior week's low, closing the week at $9.71/bu, down 1.4 percent. Brazilian harvest at 71 percent complete and running ahead of last year's pace adds to the pressure: the window for U.S. beans to capture Chinese business is narrowing by the week.

**Wheat.** Of the three markets, wheat absorbed the sharpest relative blow. U.S. ending stocks were raised 25 million bushels above February to 840 million bushels, exceeding trade expectations by 20 million, while world stocks reached 260.4 MMT — another bearish overshoot versus expectations. May SRW settled at $5.32/bu, down only 4 cents on the week, but that muted price decline masks a more concerning technical development: the close set a new contract low, confirming that the market has found no structural support. The one legitimate bullish underpinning — southern Plains dryness — has not yet been severe enough to interrupt the dominant supply narrative.

## WASDE Balance Sheet Review

### Corn

The March WASDE's corn chapter required only one line to deliver its verdict: U.S. ending stocks unchanged at 1.540 billion bushels. That number is unambiguously comfortable by historical standards — a stocks-to-use ratio of 10.2 percent — and it gave neither bulls nor bears new domestic ammunition. The meaningful number was buried deeper in the report. USDA raised world corn ending stocks to 297.4 MMT, driven by upward revisions to Brazilian production that offset modest trimming elsewhere. The trade had anticipated 295.5 MMT; the actual print was 1.9 MMT higher. For a market already carrying a near-record fund short, the surplus of expected bearish news allowed the initial 2-cent rally — short-covering, not conviction — to evaporate within minutes.

What the corn WASDE confirmed, rather than revealed, is that the global supply overhang is larger than the January or February editions suggested. The ongoing accumulation of world stocks across three consecutive WASDE releases has progressively closed the door on any price-rationing story at the global level. U.S. corn now competes in an export environment where a 297 MMT world carryout is the baseline, not a downside scenario.

**So What?** Corn merchandisers holding old-crop inventory should not read unchanged U.S. stocks as stable basis: the world stocks surprise gives importers leverage, and any basis deterioration on the Gulf over the next two weeks would be consistent with that dynamic. The Dec/Mar spread at 76 percent of full carry adequately compensates for storage, but the carry is not widening — it is at risk of compression if export demand does not materially accelerate. The better trade is to move grain against a firm Gulf basis (currently -14 cents and rallying) rather than wait for carry to do the work.

### Soybeans

Soybeans delivered the clearest fundamental signal of the three markets. USDA increased U.S. ending stocks to 390 million bushels, citing stronger-than-expected domestic crush offset by a 50-million-bushel reduction in projected exports. The export cut is both backward-looking — acknowledging that Brazilian competition has already eroded U.S. sales momentum — and forward-looking, signaling USDA's expectation that the trend continues. Trade desks had been modeling 370 million bushels of ending stocks; the 20-million-bushel miss pushed the stocks-to-use ratio above 10 percent for the first time in three years.

The world balance sheet compounded the domestic number. Global soybean ending stocks reached 124.8 MMT, with Brazil's production estimate climbing to 127 MMT — a 2 MMT upgrade that reflected the harvest pace seen in Mato Grosso and Paraná. Argentina was left at 49 MMT despite BCBA's more conservative 48.2 MMT estimate, suggesting USDA may carry additional downside risk in Argentina through the April revision. Nevertheless, the combined South American crop at approximately 176 MMT is sufficient to define the 2025/26 marketing year as one of structural surplus.

**So What?** For soybean processors with forward sales commitments, the 390 million bushel ending stock figure represents a changed structural reality, not a one-month anomaly. Domestic crush margins remain supportive — and that is the market's one genuine anchor — but basis at -38 cents under May has already softened 3 cents this week as Brazilian FOB offers continue to undercut the U.S. Gulf by $1.12 to $1.18 per bushel. Basis recovery in soybeans is unlikely to be sustained until the Brazilian harvest-induced export surge runs its course, which historically does not peak until May.

### Wheat

Wheat's WASDE chapter was unambiguous and broadly negative across both the domestic and global balance sheets. U.S. ending stocks were raised 25 million bushels to 840 million, as USDA trimmed export projections by 25 million bushels — a tacit acknowledgment that Black Sea and EU origins have displaced U.S. wheat in multiple destination markets this season. The stocks-to-use ratio expanded to 36.5 percent, a level that provides ample buffer against any weather-driven production disruption in the southern Plains.

World wheat ending stocks reached 260.4 MMT, 1.9 MMT above February and comfortably above the 258.5 MMT trade expectation. The upgrade reflected a mix of stronger supply estimates in Russia and Australia offsetting some tightening in EU. With global supplies this well-insulated, the market has no structural reason to bid prices higher unless domestic crop conditions deteriorate materially — and the Kansas dryness story, while real, has not yet reached the threshold that would compel USDA to lower its winter wheat crop condition estimate to the level where yield risk becomes inescapable.

**So What?** Flour millers who have not finished covering first-half requirements should note that this market has the technical and fundamental profile of one that continues to probe new lows. May SRW at $5.32/bu set a new contract low this week, and world stocks at 260.4 MMT are not a floor. The one scenario that revalues wheat sharply higher is a weather event in the southern Plains between now and green-up — a scenario with a non-trivial but minority probability. For merchandisers, selling wheat into any weather-driven rally is preferable to waiting for export demand to materialize on its own.

## Post-WASDE Cash & Basis

The cash market diverged sharply by commodity in the days following the March WASDE, and that divergence carries its own analytical signal.

Corn basis at the Gulf firmed 4 cents on the week to -14 cents under May — a notable countertrend move against weaker futures. Illinois River followed at -26 cents, also 2 cents firmer. The most plausible explanation is a short-term acceleration in barge bookings as commercials positioned for the next round of export inspections: with YTD corn inspections at only 35 percent of the annual USDA target and the pace running below last year, the trade is sensitive to any demand signal that interrupts the shortfall narrative. Iowa ethanol plant bids remained firm at 6 cents over May, supported by narrow corn availability in the eastern Corn Belt. The distinction between a firm ethanol market and a recovering Gulf is meaningful — both are pulling from the same thin old-crop supply base.

Soybean basis moved in the opposite direction. Gulf CIF softened 3 cents to -38 cents under May, and interior crush plant bids were essentially flat. The softening tracks directly with the WASDE-induced export reduction and the ongoing pressure from Brazilian FOB offers. At current levels, Brazilian Santos FOB is undercutting U.S. Gulf by more than a dollar per bushel, and Chinese buyers have demonstrated no urgency to shift their purchasing window back to the United States prematurely. The basis market is accurately pricing that reality.

Wheat basis was the week's clearest casualty. SRW Gulf CIF fell 5 cents to -42 cents under May, Ohio River declined 3 cents, and Midwest flour mill bids eased 2 cents. The weakness is structural, not seasonal: Black Sea origin continues to price aggressively into Asian destinations, and the WASDE's export reduction confirmed that U.S. wheat has not found the formula to compete at current price levels. Basis recovery in wheat requires either a material decline in Black Sea offers or a domestic weather premium that lifts all U.S. wheat prices — neither is imminent.

## Export & International Trade

### Corn

Weekly export inspections of 1.214 MMT for the period ending March 5 were the first encouraging corn export number in several weeks, improving 11.5 percent over the prior week's 1.089 MMT. The comparison to last year's 1.342 MMT, however, makes clear that this rebound is recovery from a weak base rather than a demand surge. Mexico absorbed 0.31 MMT, continuing its role as the anchor buyer, followed by Japan at 0.22 MMT and South Korea at 0.18 MMT. With 22.4 MMT inspected year-to-date against a 63.5 MMT annual target, the cumulative pace sits at 35 percent shipped with roughly 28 weeks remaining — a deficit that will require sustained weekly inspections above 1.5 MMT to close. The USDA's decision to maintain the export projection at 2.275 billion bushels in the March WASDE (after reducing it by 50 million) implies an expectation of stronger spring and summer demand, but that projection now requires execution to be credible.

### Soybeans

Soybean inspections of 0.842 MMT slipped from 0.914 MMT the prior week but remained above the same week last year's 0.773 MMT. China accounted for 0.41 MMT of the weekly total — a functional rate that reflects ongoing purchasing rather than aggressive accumulation. The YTD cumulative of 38.7 MMT against a 52.2 MMT annual target represents 74 percent shipped with the marketing year not yet three-quarters complete, which is structurally favorable. The challenge is that this favorable YTD position was built largely in October through January, when Brazilian competition was limited. The deceleration from here is both anticipated and already partially captured in USDA's 50-million-bushel export reduction. Weekly new export sales came in at 411,000 MT — below the expected range of 450,000 to 700,000 MT — confirming that forward booking momentum has softened as Brazilian origin becomes available.

### Wheat

Wheat inspections improved to 0.378 MMT from a weak 0.291 MMT the prior week but remained 8.2 percent below the same week last year. The Philippines took 0.11 MMT, Mexico 0.09 MMT, and Nigeria 0.07 MMT — none of them volume-moving purchases. The YTD total of 17.6 MMT against the 23.4 MMT annual target represents 75 percent shipped, a pace that is on track only if weekly inspections maintain their current level through late May. Given that Black Sea origin continues to price into Asian markets at a significant discount to U.S. Gulf offers, maintaining that inspection pace is not assured. Weekly new sales of 263,000 MT were in line with expectations but underscore the absence of any large-buyer interest in U.S. origin.

## Weather & Crop Conditions

The U.S. weather story this week belongs entirely to the southern Plains. Kansas topsoil moisture rated 41 percent short-to-very-short, and the seven-day outlook offers no meaningful relief — below-normal precipitation and above-normal temperatures are forecast through mid-March. Hard red winter wheat condition in the state has deteriorated to the point where the national rating fell one point week-over-week to 44 percent good-to-excellent, now 11 points below last year's 55 percent. The poor-to-very-poor category expanded to 21 percent, up from 18 percent last week. These numbers warrant attention but not alarm at this stage of the growing season: winter wheat in Kansas has not yet broken dormancy in most areas, which limits the immediate yield impact of the current dryness. The critical window is March green-up and April jointing, when moisture deficits translate directly into kernel count and yield.

The contrast with the northern tier is notable. Ohio and Indiana soft red winter wheat rated 58 percent good-to-excellent — well above the national average — and the outlook for those areas is more favorable heading into dormancy break. The divergence between the SRW and HRW belts is already visible in basis: SRW Gulf softened while HRW is holding relatively better, and that split could widen materially if southern Plains dryness intensifies in March.

In South America, the Brazilian soybean harvest at 71 percent complete is running five points ahead of the prior week and 13 points ahead of the same week last year. The accelerating pace eliminates any plausible weather-related supply risk for the current crop; the yield picture is set, and CONAB's 127.8 MMT estimate is broadly consistent with USDA's 127 MMT. The safrinha corn crop is a more nuanced situation: with 96 percent of the acreage planted, the critical grain-fill period falls in April, and a dry spell in Mato Grosso during that window could still introduce Brazilian corn supply risk — but that scenario is speculative at current junctures. In Argentina, recent rains of 20 to 40 millimeters in Buenos Aires province have arrested the downward spiral in corn conditions, but the improvement came too late to fully restore the crop. BCBA has trimmed its corn estimate to 49.5 MMT, and the 41 percent good-to-excellent rating — against 27 percent poor-to-very-poor — reflects a crop that has survived a stress event, not one that avoided it.

## Macro & Policy

The macro backdrop provided no directional catalyst for grain markets this week, which in the current environment constitutes its own form of negative pressure. A neutral dollar — the DXY closed at 104.2, essentially unchanged on the week — offers neither the tailwind of dollar weakness that would boost U.S. export competitiveness nor the additional headwind of a rallying dollar. At 104.2, the dollar is firm enough to keep U.S. origin at a structural disadvantage versus Brazilian and Black Sea offers in price-sensitive destination markets.

Crude oil's 2.4 percent weekly decline to $72.40 per barrel (WTI) carries indirect implications for the grain complex. Lower energy reduces ethanol blending economics at the margin, which could soften ethanol plant corn bids in the weeks ahead if crude continues to retreat. It is not a price-moving development at this week's level, but it adds a modest negative to the domestic corn demand side of the ledger. No significant U.S. agricultural policy action was announced this week that affects grain markets.

## Synthesis & Outlook

The March WASDE's triple-header of bearish surprises — higher-than-expected world corn stocks, a domestic soybean carryout that erased months of bull narrative around tight U.S. supplies, and a wheat ending stock figure that gives commercials no urgency to bid — arrived in a market where managed money was already carrying the largest aggregate short position in months. That combination should, in a mechanical sense, produce a floor: the selling is crowded and the bearish news is now on the tape. Instead, markets moved lower, which tells a more concerning story. When a market absorbs expected-to-bearish WASDE outcomes without a sustained short-covering bid, it suggests that the path of least resistance is lower — not because new sellers are aggressive, but because potential buyers have insufficient conviction to step in front of the fundamental tape.

The base-case price range for the next two to three weeks sits at $4.25 to $4.55/bu for December corn, $9.50 to $10.00/bu for November soybeans, and $5.10 to $5.50/bu for May SRW wheat. These are not ranges built around a bullish catalyst — they are built around the absence of one, with width provided by the tail risks on each side.

The bullish triggers that would break the upper bounds are specific and identifiable: a material deterioration in Kansas winter wheat conditions through March green-up that forces USDA to lower the HRW crop estimate; a disruption to the Brazilian safrinha corn crop during April grain-fill; or an unexpected surge in Chinese soybean buying that outpaces the WASDE export reduction. None of these is the base case, but any one of them would be sufficient to force violent short-covering across all three markets simultaneously — a risk that the current fund positioning makes acute.

The bearish triggers for the lower bounds are simpler: smooth Brazilian safrinha development, continued Black Sea export volume into Asian markets at discounts that shut out U.S. wheat and corn, and a corn export pace that fails to close the gap toward the annual USDA target. The dollar at 104 is not breaking anything, but a sustained move toward 106 or above would add a compounding headwind. At current positioning extremes, the asymmetric risk to a surprise bullish development — weather, geopolitics, or a policy shift — is larger than the incremental downside from another bearish WASDE in April.

## Watchlist — Week of March 16–20, 2026

- **USDA Export Inspections (Monday, March 16):** Corn needs to hold above 1.1 MMT to avoid rewidening the YTD gap. A second consecutive week above 1.2 MMT would support Gulf basis and begin rebuilding credibility in the annual export target. Watch Mexico and Japan volumes closely.

- **USDA Export Sales (Thursday, March 19):** Corn sales need to come in above 800,000 MT to maintain the annual pace. Soybean sales of 450,000 MT or higher would be a positive signal that Chinese buying has not gone entirely dormant. Wheat sales in the 250,000–350,000 MT range would be in line; a surprise above 400,000 MT would require explanation.

- **USDA Crop Progress (Monday, March 16):** The winter wheat condition rating is the number to watch. A drop below 40 percent good-to-excellent nationally, combined with an expanding poor/very poor category in Kansas, would begin to draw real weather premium into May and July SRW. Watch Kansas topsoil moisture — any improvement above 50 percent adequate would deflate the weather story.

- **CFTC Commitment of Traders (Friday, March 20, data as of March 17):** With corn's managed money short at 108,400 contracts, any meaningful reduction signals that the market is beginning to price a risk-on shift. A further build in the corn short to above 120,000 contracts would be historically extreme and would set up an increasingly volatile short-covering scenario.

- **Argentine harvest progress:** The Buenos Aires province corn harvest begins in earnest in late March. The pace and quality of early field-run estimates will either validate BCBA's 49.5 MMT or introduce further downside. Watch for any renewed dryness reports following this week's rains.

- **Brazilian safrinha development:** No report, but wire services and Mato Grosso state ag department updates will provide early reads on crop progress and early moisture conditions heading into the April grain-fill window. Any deviation from the current favorable outlook is a market-moving event.
