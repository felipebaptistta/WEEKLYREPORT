## 1. Executive Summary

**Corn**

May corn added 4.5 cents on the week to settle at $4.57/bu, a modest gain that obscures the more meaningful story unfolding in speculative positioning. Managed money turned sharply constructive in the latest CFTC report, with funds covering 42,601 short contracts while adding 24,241 new longs — a net shift that pushed the managed money position to a net long of +52,974 contracts. That repositioning is the primary driver of the price gain; the March WASDE supplied no fundamental support, leaving the U.S. corn balance sheet unchanged at 2,127 million bushels while pushing world ending stocks 3.8 MMT higher than expected to 292.8 MMT. The forward curve tells the same supply-comfortable story: the May–December spread sits at +26 cents, a clean carry structure that rewards patience over urgency. Macro energy volatility — WTI briefly spiking toward $120 before settling near $85 — introduced intraweek swings but did not change the fundamental equation.

**Soybeans**

May soybeans led the grain complex with a 14.5-cent weekly gain to $12.16/bu, driven by a convergence of signals that are rarely this well-aligned: a record January NOPA crush of 221.56 million bushels (+10.6% year-over-year), the USDA's formal acknowledgment of that crush strength through a 5-mbu March WASDE upgrade, a season-average soybean meal price raised to $300/ton, and a managed money net long of +198,902 contracts reflecting deep speculative conviction on the demand side. The Brazilian harvest at 50.6% complete is running behind last year's 60.9% pace, adding a thin layer of supply-side uncertainty that funds are positioning through. The soybean forward curve carries an important structural signal: while the nearby May–July spread remains in mild carry (+14 cents), the August–November leg has inverted sharply by 49 cents, indicating that the market is pricing a new-crop supply event — whether from a tightening U.S. carryout or demand continuity — that is not yet visible in today's balance sheet.

**Wheat**

May SRW wheat posted a nominal 1-cent gain to $5.92/bu, but the flat price belies the divergence within the wheat complex this week. The May–July spread has inverted 11 cents, an unusual structure for this time of year that reflects near-term export demand and milling procurement activity pulling on front-month wheat while the deferred market remains well-supplied. Managed money remains net short at -25,800 contracts, though the character of the positioning change is important: the weekly move was driven by long liquidation of 10,726 contracts rather than aggressive new short selling, suggesting withdrawal of bullish conviction rather than fresh bearish initiative. The global wheat balance sheet from the March WASDE showed no change to U.S. stocks (931 mbu) while reducing Australian production 1 MMT to 36.0 MMT; world ending stocks at 277.0 MMT remain historically elevated. With no USDA Crop Progress reporting active during the winter hiatus, weather-driven price signals are coming from drought monitor data — 54.9% of the contiguous U.S. is now experiencing some level of drought — rather than the weekly condition ratings the market will resume receiving in coming weeks.

**Macro / Policy**

The macro backdrop produced the most dramatic single-commodity shock of the week in crude oil, which briefly traded near $120/bbl following escalating tensions around the Strait of Hormuz before correcting sharply and settling near $85.29 — still $10.63 above the prior week's $74.66. That round trip through 14 percent of weekly volatility in crude introduced significant intraweek turbulence into the grain complex, as energy-linked commodities and risk assets broadly swung in sympathy before settling. The net result for grains is a modestly constructive macro posture: WTI at $85.29 keeps ethanol blending margins viable and supports corn demand through the energy channel, while a DXY at 98.96 — slightly softer than its recent range above 100 — provides marginal support to U.S. grain export competitiveness against competing origins. The Brazilian real weakened to 5.198 per dollar from 5.085 the prior week, a 2.2% depreciation that enhances Brazilian farmers' incentive to sell soybeans into export channels and compresses the price advantage that U.S. Gulf origin occasionally captures when the real strengthens.

**Weather**

Weather is operating as a slow-building pressure on multiple fronts simultaneously. In the United States, the drought monitor shows 54.9 percent of the contiguous country experiencing some degree of drought — a figure that has expanded materially from the prior month and now spans the Southern Plains, Oklahoma, Texas, Mississippi, and parts of the Southwest. The 6–10 day outlook calls for above-normal temperatures across the eastern half of the country and below-normal precipitation across the central Plains, a combination that deepens moisture deficits as soils begin their seasonal warm-up ahead of spring fieldwork. USDA Crop Progress reporting remains suspended for the winter; the market is reading drought conditions through the monitor rather than weekly crop condition ratings. In Brazil, soybean harvest has reached 50.6% of planted area — an improvement of nearly nine percentage points on the week but still 10.3 points behind last year's 60.9% pace. Safrinha corn planting has accelerated to 75.9%, though it trails last year's 83.1% and increasingly tight planting windows in Mato Grosso are creating logistical pressure as growers balance harvest completion against corn establishment. Argentina's outlook has stabilized following recent rainfall, with corn rated 87.4% normal-to-excellent and soybean conditions holding firm at 74% normal-to-excellent.

## 2. Corn Market Deep Dive

### Post-WASDE Balance Sheet

The March WASDE made no changes to the U.S. corn balance sheet. Production, exports, feed and residual, and ethanol use were all maintained at prior estimates, leaving ending stocks unchanged at 2,127 million bushels. The stock-to-use ratio holds at approximately 14.6 percent — comfortable by historical standards, neither tight enough to support aggressive long positioning nor loose enough to justify the large net short that managed money was carrying into the report. The absence of a domestic revision shifted all analytical weight to the global column, where the report was unambiguously bearish: world corn ending stocks were raised nearly 4 MMT above trade expectations to 292.8 MMT, driven by a 1.7 MMT production upgrade in Ukraine to 30.7 MMT, a 1.0 MMT increase in Brazil to 132.0 MMT, and partially offset by a 1.0 MMT reduction in Argentina to 52.0 MMT. The net message from the global balance sheet is that corn inventories outside the United States are expanding — which caps the price recovery available from any U.S. demand acceleration and narrows the fundamental support for a sustained rally.

### Futures & Spreads

May corn settled the week at $4.57/bu, a 4.5-cent gain that represents the net result of significant intraweek energy-driven volatility absorbed and then reversed. The December new-crop contract at $4.31/bu fell 3.5 cents, widening the May–December inverse to +26 cents — a carry structure that is logistically and financially supportive of holding old-crop corn through the spring. The full May–December spread breakdown shows May–July at +11 cents, July–September at +2.5 cents, and September–December at +14 cents. The compression of the July–September carry to just 2.5 cents is notable: it implies that the market does not see substantial incremental demand for corn in the August–September window — post-crop-pollination but pre-harvest — which is consistent with a well-supplied carryover picture. New-crop December at $4.31/bu will increasingly serve as the acreage signal for the March 31 Prospective Plantings report; the current discount to May reflects ample expected new-crop supply and gives farmers minimal financial incentive to rotate from soybeans to corn on the margin.

### Funds & Positioning

The managed money positioning data released Friday is the week's single most important corn-specific development. Funds shifted from a large net short to a net long of +52,974 contracts — a net swing of approximately 66,000 contracts — driven by two simultaneous forces: 42,601 short contracts were covered and 24,241 new long contracts were added. This is not a passive short liquidation; it is an active shift toward constructive positioning, suggesting that at least a portion of the managed money community believes the prior net short was overextended relative to the fundamental picture. The scale of the short-covering represents genuine demand, not noise. From a price-pressure standpoint, the buying associated with covering 42,601 short contracts is already reflected in the price action; what matters now is whether the 247,478 long contracts currently in the managed money book find additional justification to stay or whether they begin to unwind if the forward acreage and South American supply signals continue to weigh on new-crop. The current net long is a positioning posture that requires continued bullish confirmation to sustain — an unchanged WASDE balance sheet and rising global stocks are not that confirmation.

### Cash & Basis

Gulf corn basis is quoted at CIF +98 and FOB +115 cents over nearby futures. These are firm basis levels that reflect active export-channel demand and logistical competition for barge space on the river system. The strength of Gulf basis relative to flat price is a structurally important signal: when basis firms while futures also move higher, it indicates that commercial demand is genuine rather than purely speculative. The FOB Gulf at +115 cents is well above the levels that tend to signal export demand destruction — destination buyers at these basis levels are price-sensitive, and continued basis firmness against the backdrop of the WASDE's bearish global stocks revision implies that short-term procurement calendars are supporting physical flow. Interior basis levels (ethanol, feedlots, crush plants) were not updated this week but are assumed to reflect similar commercial firmness given the export-driven Gulf strength.

### Export Sales & Inspections

Weekly export inspections for the week ending March 5 came in at 1,517,676 MT — a decline from the prior week's 1,859,089 MT but still 18 percent above the same week last year (1,844,188 MT). The year-to-date cumulative inspection figure of 41.205 MMT now represents a substantial portion of total marketing-year shipment activity. The forward sales pipeline is robust: new corn sales for the week ending February 26 totaled 2,112.2 thousand metric tons — a strong figure that indicates active forward booking by destination buyers even against the backdrop of competitive South American origin availability. Outstanding sales of 24,958.3 TMT provide a sizeable pipeline of committed business. The combination of above-year-ago weekly inspections and a strong new-sales figure suggests that U.S. corn export demand is holding better than the global balance sheet narrative would imply, though whether it can sustain at these levels through the Northern Hemisphere spring will depend on South American corn export competition intensifying as Brazilian safrinha harvest approaches.

### Global Context

The global corn balance sheet is growing, not tightening. The March WASDE's 3.8 MMT upward surprise in world ending stocks to 292.8 MMT reflects a supply picture where South American production gains are running faster than demand absorption. Ukraine's production upgrade of 1.7 MMT to 30.7 MMT is notable because Ukrainian corn competes directly with U.S. corn in the EU, North Africa, and parts of Asia — markets where U.S. origin was capturing share earlier in the marketing year. Brazil's 132.0 MMT estimate includes a 1.0 MMT upgrade that is consistent with current safrinha planting pace, though the final figure will depend on April and May weather during grain fill in Mato Grosso. Dalian corn futures in China at approximately ¥2,264/MT reflect adequate domestic supply and limited Chinese import appetite at current price levels — a demand channel that remains structurally significant but inactive in the near term.

### So What?

Commercial operators should distinguish between the short-term barge-demand story and the structural balance sheet story, because they are currently pointing in different directions. Gulf basis at FOB +115 creates a merchandising opportunity for elevators and originators with old-crop corn priced against May, but the firmness is event-driven and reflective of current logistical tightness rather than a durable supply deficit. The managed money net long of +52,974 contracts is not a new fundamental story — it is a positioning correction from an overextended short — and it does not change the underlying global supply picture described by 292.8 MMT of world ending stocks. Hedgers evaluating new-crop December at $4.31/bu should note that the carry structure does not reward aggressive storage past the May–July window; the flat July–September spread implies the market sees no demand catalyst to pay for carrying corn through summer. Watch the March 31 Prospective Plantings report as the next fundamental reset point.

## 3. Soybean Market Deep Dive

### Post-WASDE Balance Sheet

The U.S. soybean balance sheet received a subtle but directionally important adjustment in the March WASDE. Total ending stocks were held unchanged at 350 million bushels, but the internal composition shifted: crush demand was raised 5 million bushels while imports were increased 5 million bushels to supply that additional processing activity. The offsetting nature of these adjustments preserved the headline stocks number, but the signal embedded in the crush increase is the more important analytical point — USDA is formally acknowledging that domestic processing demand is running above its prior estimate, consistent with the record NOPA data discussed below. On the supply side, Argentina's soybean production was trimmed 0.5 MMT to 48.0 MMT, reflecting localized yield losses from earlier dryness. The season-average soybean meal price was raised to $300 per ton, a USDA acknowledgment of the processing sector's pricing power — and a signal that the demand-driven bull case in soybeans is grounded in observable current-season data, not speculation about future supply.

### Futures & Spreads

May soybeans at $12.16/bu represent the strongest closing price in the soybean complex in several weeks, with the 14.5-cent weekly gain leading the grain complex. November new-crop at $11.58/bu posted a more modest 4.5-cent gain, widening the May–November old-crop/new-crop spread to +58 cents. The spread structure carries a critical message: the nearby portion of the curve (May–July at +14 cents) remains in normal carry, indicating that old-crop procurement demand is active but not frantic. The inversion that begins in the July–August spread (-15 cents) and deepens sharply through August–November (-49 cents) is the analytically unusual feature of this week's market. A 49-cent inverse from August to November implies the market is pricing a demand event or supply constraint that peaks in the pre-harvest period and then resolves with new-crop arrivals. This structure is consistent with a domestic crush sector operating at very high utilization rates through summer, drawing down old-crop supplies faster than a headline balance sheet might suggest, before new-crop relief arrives in the fall. It is also a spread that punishes speculative storage of forward soybeans and rewards processors who lock in meal sales now against current soybean ownership.

### Products & Crush

The soybean products complex is providing the most durable structural support in the grain complex this week. January NOPA crush at 221.56 million bushels set a record for the month of January and came in 10.57 percent above January 2025's 200.30 mbu. Compared to December's record 224.99 mbu, the January figure represents a modest seasonal decline of 1.5 percent — normal given slightly fewer processing days — rather than any erosion in underlying demand. Soybean oil stocks rose to 1.900 billion pounds from 1.642 billion in December, a 15.7 percent inventory build that reflects strong oil production running alongside the elevated crush pace. The current NOPA pace through January is running approximately 7.2 percent above last year on a full marketing-year basis, materially outrunning the WASDE's implied crush growth assumption of approximately 5 percent. If NOPA crush continues at this pace through the next NOPA release on March 16 (covering February activity), USDA will face mounting pressure to upgrade its full-year crush estimate further in the April WASDE. Soybean meal is the primary demand driver: meal prices are strong enough ($300/ton season average per USDA) to incentivize processors to run at maximum capacity, and this is self-reinforcing — higher crush volumes support soybean demand, which tightens the old-crop balance sheet and validates the spread inversion noted above. Ethanol production at 1.095 million barrels per day (most recent EIA data, Feb 27) provides the secondary cross-commodity demand signal, though its relevance to soybeans is indirect through the corn complex.

### Funds & Positioning

Managed money's net long of +198,902 contracts in soybeans is the largest speculative long position in the grain complex by a substantial margin. The latest weekly move added 21,865 new long contracts while shorts increased by a smaller 7,165 — a configuration of both fresh longs and fresh shorts that indicates a two-sided market with conviction on both sides, rather than a one-directional momentum trade. With 239,399 long contracts and only 40,497 short contracts, the long-to-short ratio in managed money positioning stands at approximately 5.9:1 — historically elevated and reflecting a level of bullish speculative conviction that creates meaningful vulnerability if the fundamental picture deteriorates. At the same time, the fundamental picture this week — record crush, WASDE crush upgrade, firm meal prices, and a harvest-trailing Brazil — is genuinely supportive of the long. The risk embedded in this positioning is not the direction but the magnitude: a position of this size can move prices sharply when unwinding begins, and trigger points (a NOPA miss, an acceleration in Brazilian harvest, or a resolution of the Strait of Hormuz situation reducing commodity risk premium) are all plausible in the next 30 days.

### Cash & Basis

U.S. Gulf soybean basis at CIF +105 and FOB +128 cents over nearby futures represents firm absolute levels that reflect the crush sector's and exporter's willingness to pay up for nearby physical beans. The FOB Gulf at +128 is notable: Brazilian Santos FOB offers for similar-month shipment would need to be meaningfully below this level to attract switching from long-committed buyers. The USD/BRL at 5.198 — a 2.2% depreciation in the real from the prior week — enhances Brazilian farmers' revenue per dollar of soybeans sold, which increases their sell-side motivation at current R$/bag prices but also raises the dollar cost of Brazilian origin to U.S.-equivalent buyers, partially closing the price gap that normally favors Brazilian origin during harvest season. Interior crush plant basis (Iowa, Illinois) was not updated this week but is assumed firm given the level of processor demand visible in NOPA data.

### Export Sales & Inspections

Soybean export inspections for the week ending March 5 totaled 879,190 MT, a decline from the prior week's 1,162,033 MT but 2.5 percent above the same week last year (857,466 MT). The year-to-date cumulative figure of 27.086 MMT is below the comparable period in prior years, reflecting the typical pattern of U.S. soybean exports front-loading in the September–January period before giving way to South American origin. Weekly export sales for the week ending February 26 showed 393.8 thousand metric tons in new soybean sales and 1,119.5 TMT in actual exports — the gap between new sales (forward bookings) and exports (physical shipments) is narrowing, consistent with the seasonal transition from pipeline-filling to execution. Outstanding soybean sales of 9,880.6 TMT represent a sizeable but not excessive forward commitment. The pace of new sales at 393.8 TMT is below the peak booking rates of the fall, reflecting normal seasonal deceleration as Brazilian harvest competes, but it does not indicate demand destruction — it indicates that buyers who needed coverage largely obtained it earlier in the marketing year.

### Global Context

Brazil is the dominant variable in the global soybean supply picture, and the current harvest data frames the risk more conservatively than typical for this time of year. At 50.6% complete — running 10.3 percentage points behind last year's 60.9% pace — the harvest has been slower due to intermittent wet conditions, particularly in the Médio-Norte region of Mato Grosso. The forecast for significant rainfall beginning around March 15 could further delay field operations and introduce additional moisture-related quality concerns. CONAB's current production estimate of approximately 127 MMT for Brazilian soybeans reflects a large crop that, when fully exported, will exert structural downward pressure on U.S. soybean export demand for the March–August period. However, the slower harvest creates a short-term window in which Brazilian export availability is constrained, U.S. crush demand is running at record pace, and the market is appropriately pricing a premium for near-term availability. Argentina's 48.0 MMT soybean crop (USDA, -0.5 MMT in March WASDE) is still developing, with 27% of first-crop soybeans in grain fill and 40% of second-crop facing continued moisture constraints in parts of the core production zone.

### So What?

The soybean market this week is genuinely two-sided in a way that the flat price alone does not communicate. The domestic crush story — record NOPA, firm meal prices, USDA recognizing the demand — is real, durable, and fundamentally bullish for old-crop. The Brazilian harvest story — large crop, behind schedule but catching up, eventually delivering into export channels — is also real and eventually bearish for the May forward curve. Processors with crush coverage running through June should feel well-positioned; those without coverage into the July–August window are exposed to the spread inversion that prices that period at 15–49 cents above November. Commercial operators pricing old-crop soybeans should treat current May at $12.16 as supported by genuine demand rather than speculative excess, which means the appropriate action depends on whether the producer or elevator is selling against meal-and-oil margin or against a flat-price view. If selling against a meal margin, the current price is defensible. If selling on a flat-price basis with no offsetting product hedge, the 49-cent August–November inverse implies that the carry available from holding beans is largely already extracted by the market.

## 4. Wheat Market Deep Dive

### Post-WASDE Balance Sheet

The March WASDE left the U.S. wheat balance sheet unchanged at 931 million bushels of ending stocks, maintaining the existing export, domestic use, and production estimates. USDA did raise the season-average price by five cents to $4.95/bu, a minor upward adjustment that acknowledges firmness in nearby cash wheat markets but does not signal a fundamental tightening in supply. The global picture saw one notable production reduction: Australia's 2025/26 wheat crop was trimmed 1.0 MMT to 36.0 MMT, reflecting post-harvest loss assessment. Global wheat ending stocks at 277.0 MMT remain historically elevated, and the Australian reduction had no material impact on the overall supply-comfortable narrative. USDA's decision to hold U.S. exports unchanged despite competitive pressure from Black Sea and EU origins reflects either a confidence in the current shipping pace or a willingness to revisit if the weekly inspection data deteriorates in coming months.

### Futures & Spreads

May SRW wheat at $5.92/bu posted a nominal 1-cent weekly gain that masks the most structurally unusual feature in the wheat spread complex this week: the May–July spread has inverted by 11 cents. At this time of year — March, well ahead of Northern Hemisphere winter wheat harvest — a nearby futures inverse is uncommon and carries a specific message: front-month buyers are willing to pay more for immediate delivery than for delivery two months forward. This can reflect a combination of factors including near-term milling procurement demand, export-channel loading requirements against outstanding commitments, and the absence of comfortable storage incentives for holders of physical wheat. The inversion then resolves into deferred carry: July–September is +11 cents and September–December is +14 cents, suggesting that once the nearby procurement demand is satisfied, the market reverts to a supply-comfortable carry structure. Watching whether the May–July inverse deepens or narrows as the contract approaches first notice day will be informative about the persistence of the near-term demand signal.

### Funds & Positioning

Managed money's net short in CBOT SRW wheat stands at -25,800 contracts, a position that is meaningful but significantly smaller in absolute magnitude than the corn and soybean long positions. The character of this week's positioning change is worth examining: the move was driven by long liquidation of 10,726 contracts rather than new short selling (-2,224 contracts). This distinction matters. Long liquidation does not reflect new bearish conviction — it reflects existing longs abandoning positions, which is typically associated with a loss of confidence in the bullish thesis rather than the establishment of a new bearish one. The combined result is a managed money positioning picture where the spec community is withdrawing from directional wheat exposure rather than actively pressing a view. That posture — neither aggressively short nor building new longs — is consistent with a market searching for its next fundamental catalyst, which in wheat will most likely come from either a weather event in the U.S. winter wheat belt (when Crop Progress reporting resumes) or a shift in global export flow data.

### Cash & Basis

Wheat basis data for this week was not separately updated in the current data pack. The prior basis framework — Gulf SRW at -42¢ under May, Ohio River at -35¢, and Midwest flour mill at -28¢ — was established in the context of a bearish flat-price environment and Black Sea competition; with May futures now 60 cents higher at $5.92, the absolute cash price has improved. The May–July inverse noted above suggests that nearby physical demand is active enough to pull premium above deferred delivery, which would normally translate into firmer near-term basis at export and interior locations. Flour millers seeking coverage ahead of the spring baking season are a likely source of the inverse, as they seek fixed-price grain against forward flour sales commitments. This dynamic is constructive for nearby basis even against a supply-comfortable balance sheet.

### Export Sales & Inspections

Weekly export inspections for wheat totaled 496,108 MT for the week ending March 5 — a 40 percent improvement from the prior week's 354,518 MT and, more significantly, 105 percent above the same week last year (241,646 MT). Year-to-date wheat inspections stand at 19.124 MMT. The year-over-year outperformance in wheat inspections this week is the strongest relative performance of the three commodities and deserves specific attention: U.S. wheat is moving through export channels at a rate roughly double last year's pace in comparable weeks. Whether this reflects a structural shift in U.S. wheat competitiveness, a specific destination-country procurement surge, or front-loading against uncertainty remains to be seen, but the data is directionally constructive for the wheat export outlook. Weekly export sales for the week ending February 26 showed 242.6 TMT in new sales — modest relative to the inspections pace, suggesting that the current strong shipment pace is drawing down previously booked commitments (outstanding sales of 4,732.2 TMT) rather than adding new business to the pipeline.

### Global Context

The global wheat market is well-supplied — 277.0 MMT of world ending stocks is historically elevated — but the market is not static. Australia's 1.0 MMT production reduction to 36.0 MMT is post-harvest confirmation of yield losses, and while the impact on global totals is modest, it reduces the Australian export surplus that competes with U.S. origin in Asian milling markets. Black Sea export competition, particularly from Russia, remains the primary structural pressure on U.S. wheat market share in North Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East. EU Euronext wheat at approximately €196.50/MT ($214/MT equivalent) is competitive against U.S. SRW in most European-adjacent markets. The constructive element in the global context is the drought monitor data: the same dryness affecting U.S. Plains is part of a broader Northern Hemisphere pattern, and the upcoming U.S. winter wheat growing season will be watched closely once Crop Progress reporting resumes for any yield impairment that could shift the global supply calculus in 2026/27.

### So What?

The nearby May–July inverse in wheat is the trade to watch this week. It represents a near-term demand signal in a commodity that has otherwise been characterized by comfortable supply and bearish fund positioning. Flour millers and food-grade wheat users who have not fully covered their spring–summer requirements should treat the current nearby strength as a procurement window — the inverse suggests that the market is rewarding early coverage more than deferred purchase. Commercial elevators holding physical SRW wheat priced against May futures should be attentive to the roll period: rolling from May into July means receiving the 11-cent inverse (paying up), which changes the economics of carry decisions. Hedgers evaluating new-crop winter wheat seeding decisions for fall 2026 should note that while the balance sheet is ample globally, the drought developing across the southern Plains creates a weather-based optionality in HRW wheat that the current flat price does not yet fully price.

## 6. International Cash & Trade Flows

Brazilian soybean export competitiveness remains structurally dominant in the global trade picture despite the harvest pace running behind last year. Santos and Paranaguá FOB soybean offers have been quoted at varying discounts to U.S. Gulf origin, but the USD/BRL depreciation to 5.198 this week — a 2.2% move against the dollar — compresses the real-denominated farm-gate price that Brazilian farmers receive for each metric ton exported and simultaneously makes Brazilian origin more expensive in dollar terms to destination buyers. The net effect is a narrowing of the U.S.–Brazil origin spread that partially explains why U.S. soybean inspections held above year-ago levels even as Brazilian harvest progresses. If the BRL continues to weaken, the competitive discount of Brazilian origin relative to U.S. Gulf narrows further, providing additional window for U.S. export execution.

Argentine corn export competition will expand through April and May as harvest progresses beyond its current 7.2% completion rate. Current average yields of 9.5 MT/ha in the North Core region and 6.5 MT/ha in eastern Entre Ríos suggest that Argentina's 57 MMT corn crop will emerge in the global market as a substantial export supply in the second quarter. Brazilian safrinha corn competition will follow in the June–August window as that crop is harvested. Together, South American corn will progressively compete with U.S. origin in Asian destination markets, though the near-term U.S. advantage remains intact as long as Brazilian safrinha corn is still in the field.

Black Sea wheat continues to define the competitive ceiling for U.S. wheat export pricing in North Africa and Asian milling markets, but this week's inspection data showing U.S. wheat shipments running 105% above year-ago volumes suggests that U.S. origin is finding buyers either at price-competitive levels or in markets where origin preference plays a role. The EU Euronext wheat at €196.50/MT ($214 equivalent) is broadly competitive with U.S. SRW at current futures and exchange rate levels.

## 9. Weather & Crop Conditions

**United States**

USDA Crop Progress reporting remains suspended for the winter season. USDA publishes State Stories during this period instead of the weekly condition ratings that the market will resume receiving in coming weeks. In the absence of condition data, the drought monitor is the primary weather instrument for U.S. agricultural weather assessment. As of early March, approximately 54.9 percent of the contiguous United States is experiencing some level of drought — a meaningful increase from the prior month's readings — with dryness concentrated across Mississippi, Oklahoma, Texas, and parts of the Southwest. The 6–10 day outlook calls for above-normal temperatures across the eastern half of the country and the Southern Plains, accompanied by below-normal precipitation for the central Plains. This temperature and precipitation combination during the critical late-winter period creates accumulating moisture deficits in winter wheat soils and delays spring fieldwork preparation. The Northern Plains and portions of the Mountain West are expected to receive above-normal precipitation, a split that highlights the regional specificity of the drought risk. Snowpack in parts of the West remains below normal, adding water supply concerns for irrigated acreage in the spring.

**Brazil**

Soybean harvest in Brazil has reached 50.6% of planted area, an improvement of 8.9 percentage points from the prior week's 41.7%, but still 10.3 points behind last year's 60.9% pace and only marginally ahead of the 5-year average of 48.5%. The harvest advance has been uneven: central Mato Grosso and parts of Goiás have moved faster, while the Médio-Norte region of Mato Grosso has seen localized interruptions from excess moisture. A significant rainfall event is forecast to begin around March 15 and continue through at least March 19, potentially disrupting field operations and increasing the risk of grain damage from persistent humidity. Pest and disease pressure is elevated in high-moisture areas. Safrinha corn planting has reached 75.9% nationally, accelerating by 11 percentage points in a single week but remaining 7.2 points behind last year's 83.1% pace. Late-planted safrinha fields in Mato Grosso will have a compressed grain-fill window in April if dry season conditions arrive on the normal schedule.

**Argentina**

Argentina's weather outlook has improved materially following recent rainfall events across key production zones. Precipitation totaling 20–40mm reached northwest Argentina, central Santa Fe, southern Córdoba, and western Buenos Aires province, replenishing soil moisture that had been depleted during February's dryness. Corn development is broadly favorable: 87.4% of the crop is rated between normal and excellent, with early harvest yielding approximately 9.5 MT/ha in the North Core region and 6.5 MT/ha in eastern Entre Ríos. Total corn production is projected near 57 MMT. Soybean conditions are stable at 74% normal-to-excellent, though 40% of second-crop soybeans remain in yield-definition stages that still carry moisture sensitivity. Production is currently estimated near 48.5 MMT. The rainfall timing — arriving as soybeans entered grain fill — was beneficial, though late for the earliest-planted fields.

## 10. Synthesis & Outlook

The week of March 11, 2026 presents a grain complex in which the dominant price signal is coming from the fund positioning repositioning rather than from the fundamental balance sheet, and the macro backdrop — specifically the WTI crude volatility event — amplified that repositioning into price action that exceeded what the WASDE data alone could justify. The March WASDE was directionally constructive for soybeans (crush upgrade, meal price upgrade) and neutral to modestly bearish for corn and wheat (global corn stocks 3.8 MMT above expectations; wheat unchanged). Yet corn gained 4.5 cents, soybeans gained 14.5 cents, and wheat gained 1 cent. The answer lies in the CFTC data: managed money added net long exposure in corn (+66,000 contract swing) and soybeans, while withdrawing exposure in wheat. The macro energy spike into $120 oil added a commodity risk premium that was subsequently partially reversed as WTI settled at $85.29.

The most important distinction in forming a forward view is between the demand-led bull case in soybeans and the positioning-led bull case in corn. Soybeans have genuine fundamental backing: record NOPA crush, USDA recognizing the crush strength, firm meal prices, and a harvest that is running behind last year's pace, creating near-term supply tightness. This is a market where higher prices can be argued on the merits. Corn's net long of +52,974 contracts is primarily a function of prior short-covering rather than new demand discovery; the global balance sheet expanded in the March WASDE and the domestic one was unchanged. The corn bull case requires additional fundamental support — either an acceleration of export pace, a demand catalyst from the energy complex, or a weather event — to sustain the net long position at current price levels.

Wheat occupies a distinct position: structurally well-supplied globally (277.0 MMT), with a smaller fund position (net short -25,800) being reduced through long liquidation rather than short selling, and a nearby spread structure that has inverted 11 cents — an unusual feature that bears watching. The absence of Crop Progress reporting means that U.S. drought conditions are developing without the weekly market feedback mechanism that typically processes weather risk in real time. When Crop Progress resumes, the market may need to rapidly incorporate drought information that has been accumulating without a pricing mechanism.

**Key themes for the week of March 16–20:**
- NOPA February crush report (March 16): whether the record crush pace is sustained will either reinforce or undermine the soybean demand bull case that is currently driving +198,902 contracts of managed money long exposure
- Brazilian harvest progress: the forecast rain beginning March 15 could slow the harvest advance and tighten near-term export availability, providing additional near-term soybean support
- March 31 Prospective Plantings: acreage intentions for corn and soybeans will set the new-crop balance sheet expectations; current new-crop December corn at $4.31/bu versus November soybeans at $11.58/bu provides a strong soy/corn price ratio that favors soybean planting
- U.S. drought evolution: with the 6–10 day outlook calling for above-normal temperatures and below-normal precipitation in the central Plains, drought expansion heading into the Crop Progress restart will determine whether a weather premium needs to be built into HRW wheat
- Macro crude oil: WTI at $85.29 is a stabilizing level for grain demand; a second spike toward $120 would inject renewed commodity risk premium, while a correction toward $70 would reduce ethanol support and dampen the macro-driven positioning bids
