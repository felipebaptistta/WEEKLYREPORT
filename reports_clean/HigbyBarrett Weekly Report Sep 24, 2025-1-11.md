Macro/Policy – Softer Dollar, Divergent South American Currencies Shape Trade

The U.S. dollar index eased to about 97.3 after the Federal Reserve’s rate cut, marginally improving U.S.
competitiveness. Brazil’s real strengthened to 5.30 per dollar, discouraging farmer selling and slowing
corn and soybean exports, while Argentina’s peso collapsed to about 1,412 per dollar on the official
market and around 1,475 on the parallel market, triggering a wave of farmer sales during the tax holiday.
Crude oil held steady between $63 and $64 per barrel, sustaining favorable ethanol and biodiesel
margins, while U.S. Treasury yields slipped below 4.1 percent, trimming grain storage costs.

Weather

U.S. harvest benefitted from warm, dry conditions across much of the Midwest, accelerating corn and
soybean maturity while leaving subsoil deficits severe in the western Corn Belt. A light frost in North
Dakota and northern Minnesota caused minor, localized damage. In South America, Brazil’s soybean
planting awaited consistent rains in central regions, while dryness in southern Brazil raised wheat
concerns. Argentina remained moisture-limited across the Pampas despite some showers, leaving
planting conditions less than ideal.

grain inspections.

and update wheat production.

Export Sales: Sep 25 report will confirm whether Argentina’s surge is displacing U.S. corn and soy;
China’s absence from U.S. purchases remains a key risk.

Logistics: Critically low Mississippi River levels keep barge freight inflated – any rains or Army Corps
measures could ease pressure.

South America: Brazil needs consistent rains by early October to accelerate soybean planting, while
Argentine sowing hinges on financing and weather after the tax holiday.

Black Sea & Policy: Russian export pace and Argentina’s campaign rhetoric will shape global trade

Corn Market Deep Dive

Futures & Spreads: December 2025 corn futures were stable this week, settling at $4.24/bu on
September 24, down just 2¢ from the prior week. The market traded quietly, with five-day realized
volatility near 11 percent, reflecting a balance between harvest pressure and steady demand. Calendar
spreads widened modestly, with Dec ’25–Mar ’26 at +19¢ (61 percent of full carry) versus 17¢ a week
earlier, signaling normal commercial storage incentives without tipping into surplus-driven maximum
carry. Options activity was subdued, with implied volatility near 20 percent and skew narrowing as traders
anticipated rangebound trade.

Positioning & Money Flow: Short covering helped support values. CFTC Commitments of Traders data
(as of September 16, published September 19) showed managed money trimming net shorts to roughly –
81,500 contracts, the least bearish stance since May. Commercials simultaneously added hedges as
new-crop corn filled elevators, lifting total open interest by about 50,000 contracts. This mix reflects
commercials locking in storage carries while funds eased off heavy shorts, leaving the market less
vulnerable to downside momentum but also lacking speculative buying power.

U.S. Cash & Basis: Cash corn showed a split pattern. At the Gulf, CIF premiums for September loadings
firmed to +79¢ over December, but high barge freight costs forced FOB offers lower, easing to about
+99¢ over December ($243/MT) from +105¢ the prior week. Interior river elevators tracked the freight
pinch, with Mid-Mississippi and Ohio Valley bids falling 5–10¢ as drafts were cut and loadings delayed.
By contrast, ethanol plants and feeders held firm: Indiana and Ohio processors stayed near even to +5¢
over December, while western Corn Belt plants paid –20¢ to –10¢, historically strong for harvest. The
national corn basis index averaged –22¢ under December, near seasonal norms.

Export Flows: Exports remain supportive. Weekly inspections (week ending September 18, reported
September 22) reached 1.329 MMT, down 12 percent from the prior week but 12 percent above last year.
USDA confirmed two flash sales to Mexico totaling 443,000 MT, highlighting Mexico’s role as a consistent
buyer. New-crop export sales (week ending September 18, reported September 25) totaled about

With funds no longer heavily short, the market is balanced but lacks speculative buying. Rangebound
trade around $4.20 looks likely into the September 30 Quarterly Stocks report, which is the next catalyst
for direction.

Soybean Market Deep Dive

Futures & Spreads: November 2025 soybean futures fell sharply this week, losing 34¾¢ (–3.3 percent)
to settle at $10.09/bu on September 24. The selloff was triggered by Argentina’s surprise suspension of
export taxes through October, which sent a wave of farmer selling onto the market. Chinese crushers
immediately booked an estimated 10 Argentine cargoes, sidelining U.S. origin and pushing U.S. export
inspections down more than 40 percent week-on-week to 484,000 MT. The futures curve weakened
modestly, with the Nov ’25–Jan ’26 spread widening to 14–15¢ (60–65 percent of full carry). This
structure reflects heavy early harvest deliveries offset by strong crush demand, preventing a collapse into
maximum carry.

Fundamentals & Trade: The tax holiday in Argentina, combined with a collapsing peso, made Argentine
soybeans and products the cheapest globally, undercutting both U.S. and Brazilian offers. Soymeal FOB
Rosario fell $25/MT below U.S. Gulf, while whole beans on Matba-Rofex surged as exporters rushed to
register shipments before duties return. In Brazil, FOB Santos premiums rose to +250¢ over November
as competition shifted toward Argentine meal and oil, leaving more bean demand to Brazil. In the U.S.,
Gulf bids softened 5–10¢ under the weight of barge freight costs and South American competition. The
national soybean basis averaged –43¢ under November futures, among the weakest harvest-time levels
in years, though processors continued to pay premiums thanks to robust crush margins.

Positioning & Money Flow: Managed money funds responded to the policy shock by liquidating longs,
with open interest in soybeans falling about 10,000 contracts as speculators pulled back. Unlike corn,
where funds reduced shorts, soybeans saw outright long liquidation. Commercial hedgers added shorts
as harvest pressure mounted and basis weakened. Implied volatility climbed modestly (realized vol near
15 percent), underscoring uncertainty about whether Argentina’s export surge is a temporary shock or a
structural shift.

So What? The soybean market has transitioned abruptly from a demand-driven equilibrium into one
dominated by South American supply shocks. With Argentina flooding the market during its tax holiday,
U.S. futures and basis are under pressure despite robust domestic crush demand. The risk is asymmetric:

tightening spreads and firming futures. Until then, U.S. soy remains trapped in a weak export
environment, with November futures anchored near $10. Short-term direction hinges on whether China
steps in for U.S. purchases despite tariffs or continues to lean entirely on Brazil and Argentina.

Wheat Market Deep Dive

Futures & Spreads: CBOT December 2025 wheat futures extended their slide, losing 8¾¢ (–1.7
percent) to close at $5.19½/bu on September 24. The downtrend reflects persistent global oversupply,
with ample production from Russia, Canada, and the EU offsetting any regional concerns. While rallies
remain shallow and fleeting, spreads suggest an orderly market rather than one in distress. The Dec ’25–
Mar ’26 carry widened slightly to 18¢, equal to 50–55 percent of full carry. This level signals adequate
commercial hedging activity and comfortable storage returns, but not the burdensome surpluses that
would push spreads toward maximum carry.

Global Supply & Demand: Large Northern Hemisphere crops continue to weigh on futures. Russian
export offers remain aggressive, with 12.5 percent protein wheat holding near $255/MT FOB, keeping
pressure on U.S. HRW and SRW in key tenders. Canada and the EU both raised crop estimates, while
Australia’s crop prospects steadied despite dryness. Together, these upgrades reinforce the global
abundance theme. U.S. export inspections were 0.85 MMT for the week, solid compared with last year,
but still insufficient to offset stiff Black Sea competition.

Cash & Basis: In the U.S. cash market, SRW Gulf basis stayed firm near +80¢ over December as
traders covered prior sales, while HRW FOB Texas Gulf strengthened by 14¢ to ~78½¢ under December,
reflecting exporter buying after harvest. Interior basis was steady, with mills largely covered for fall needs.
Quality concerns linger in some spring wheat regions, but with global supplies so heavy, they have not
materially altered trade flows.

Speculative Positioning: Managed money remains heavily net short across wheat contracts, with
Chicago SRW attracting the bulk of bearish exposure. The persistence of this large short base is both a
weight on prices and a latent source of volatility. Funds have consistently re-established shorts on rallies,
confident that abundant global supply caps upside. Yet this short exposure leaves the market primed for
sharp squeezes on any bullish catalyst, whether logistical disruption, weather shock, or geopolitical event.
Commercial hedgers have taken the other side, steadily layering in forward sales. As a result, open
interest has crept higher while the forward curve remains in carry, underscoring the dominance of
commercial storage hedging.

So What? Wheat markets remain trapped in a bearish fundamental backdrop, but event-driven volatility is
a clear risk. On one hand, abundant supply, with Russia, Canada, and the EU all flush with exportable
wheat, suggests rallies will be sold and futures will stay defensive near the $5.20 area. On the other,
prices are now low enough to stimulate incremental demand: U.S. exports are running ahead of last year,
and cheaper wheat is drawing into feed channels. This value-driven buying places a floor under the
market, limiting downside. For now, expect wheat to grind in a narrow range with occasional sharp rallies
when shorts are forced to cover. The key is that futures are no longer trading on weather or crop size
alone, instead, the battle is between entrenched bearish supply expectations and slowly improving
demand. Until a fundamental tightening materializes, wheat will remain rangebound, with risk skewed
toward choppy, event-driven price action.

U.S. Cash Market & Basis

Corn basis showed a split pattern this week. At the Gulf, CIF barge corn premiums firmed to +79¢ over
Dec for September loadings, reflecting the scramble for barge capacity amid record freight costs. Yet the
high transportation expense forced FOB Gulf offers lower, easing to about +99¢ over Dec ($243/MT)
versus +105¢ the prior week. This dynamic left exporters passing freight burdens back to farmers through
weaker basis. Interior river elevators tracked the freight pinch, with some bids in the Mid-Mississippi and
Ohio valleys falling 5–10¢ as barge drafts were cut and loadings delayed. In contrast, ethanol plants and
feeders in the interior maintained relatively firm bids: Indiana and Ohio processors held steady near even
to +5¢ over Dec, and some western Corn Belt plants were paying –20¢ to –10¢, historically strong for
harvest. The cmdtyView national corn basis index averaged –22¼¢ under Dec, slightly firmer than the
prior week but near seasonal norms.

Soybean basis weakened broadly on harvest pressure and export softness. The Gulf basis slipped 5–10¢
as U.S. offers lost ground to Argentina’s heavily discounted beans after the tax holiday. Nationally,
soybean basis averaged –43¢ under November futures, among the weakest early-harvest levels in years.
River locations were especially pressured by the freight spike, with Upper Mississippi elevators cutting
bids sharply. By contrast, domestic processors maintained firm bids thanks to strong crush margins:
board crush values stayed historically high, and some Western Corn Belt crushers paid up to +10¢ over
Nov to secure supplies even as harvest deliveries expanded. This divergence underscores the tension
between robust domestic demand and weak export competitiveness.

Wheat basis showed modest firmness in select channels. Gulf SRW remained relatively strong near +80¢
over Dec, reflecting traders covering prior export commitments. HRW FOB Texas Gulf improved by ~14¢
on the week, now quoted at 78½¢ under Dec, as exporters replenished nearby coverage after harvest.
Interior basis was steady, with mills largely covered for fall needs.

So What? The cash market is reflecting the same themes evident in futures spreads. Corn and wheat are
in carry, with exporters hedging forward and elevators capturing storage returns, while soybeans are
under immediate pressure from Argentina’s surge. Merchandisers face weak export basis but firm
domestic demand in both corn and soy, creating a fragmented market where processors and feeders are
better buyers than exporters. For producers, corn and wheat economics favor storage and hedging into
deferred months, while soybeans may warrant quicker sales into strong domestic crusher bids rather than
holding grain amid weak river values.

Weather & Crop Conditions

United States: Conditions shifted toward a harvest-friendly pattern. Warm, dry weather across much of
the Midwest accelerated corn and soybean maturity, keeping national harvest progress near the five-year
average. Dryness persisted in the western Corn Belt, leaving subsoil deficits severe and raising concerns
about stalk integrity and lodging risk as harvest advances. A light frost touched northern North Dakota
and Minnesota, with lows in the 30s°F, but most crops were mature enough to avoid material damage.
Scattered rains in the central and eastern Corn Belt provided some short-term soil moisture relief for
winter wheat planting, but not enough to lift critically low Mississippi River levels.

South America: Brazil’s planting season is about to ramp up, but central regions (Mato Grosso, Goiás)
remained dry under El Niño influence, delaying widespread soybean seeding until consistent rains arrive.
Southern Brazil (Rio Grande do Sul) also trended dry, adding stress to the wheat crop at a sensitive
stage, with implications for both yield and quality. In Argentina, soil moisture remained limited across the
Pampas despite some showers in northern provinces, leaving conditions less than ideal ahead of October
corn and soybean planting. The temporary removal of export taxes spurred farmer sales, but the incentive
to accelerate planting still hinges on improved rainfall and access to inputs.

Global Weather Issues: Canada’s spring wheat and canola crops were revised higher after favorable
late-season rains, while Russia and the EU both lifted grain production estimates, reinforcing global
abundance. In contrast, dryness in southern Brazil and Argentina remains a watchpoint, particularly for
wheat yields and upcoming corn and soybean planting. Australia’s outlook stayed dry under El Niño,
weighing on wheat prospects, while Ukraine’s late-summer rains improved soil conditions for winter
sowing.

So what: Weather has transitioned from being a yield risk to shaping harvest pace and logistics in the
U.S., with dryness now a factor for river flows and basis rather than production. The light frost scare
confirmed that major yield threats are mostly behind. Globally, the focus shifts south: delays in Brazil’s
soybean planting or persistent dryness in Argentina could tighten early 2026 export availability and add
risk premium back into soybean and corn markets later this fall. For now, favorable U.S. harvest weather
is slightly bearish for prices, while South American uncertainty provides the next potential catalyst.

This week marked the clear shift from weather risk to harvest reality. Futures and cash markets are
aligned: modestly wider carries in corn, soy, and wheat correspond with ample but orderly supply
expectations, while cash basis patterns reinforce the split between weak river/export values and firm
domestic processor demand. The market is not signaling surplus panic, spreads remain well below full
carry, but harvest pressure is evident, especially in soybeans where Argentina’s tax holiday added an
external supply shock.

Speculative positioning also reflects this transition. Funds have cut shorts in corn and wheat, reducing
downside momentum, while soybeans saw outright long liquidation. This leaves markets in a balanced
but sensitive posture: with fewer heavy shorts, a fresh rally will require a true fundamental catalyst (such
as disappointing yield reports or unexpected export demand), while the absence of such catalysts could
see the trade lean bearish again.

Base case: Rangebound trade is the most likely near term. December corn is expected to hover between
$4.10–$4.40, November soybeans around $10.00, and December wheat near $5.20. Spreads are likely to
hold at 50–65% of full carry as commercials continue to hedge storage, and basis should stay bifurcated
firm at processors, weak along the river.

Bullish triggers: A surprise in next week’s Sept 30 Quarterly Stocks or early October WASDE, such as a
lower-than-expected corn yield (175 bpa or below) or a sharp trim to soybean output, could tighten
balance sheets and spark short-covering. Any significant Chinese soybean purchase, or a continuation of
Mexico’s large-scale corn buying, would add fuel. On wheat, any sign of Russian export restriction could
trigger a short squeeze given heavy fund shorts. Improvements in Mississippi River levels would also
allow exports to flow more freely, supporting Gulf basis and narrowing spreads.

Bearish triggers: Conversely, if harvest conditions remain ideal and early yield results trend better than
feared, markets could resume their grind lower. In corn, a strong harvest pace and yields near USDA’s
current 177.5 bpa would pull Dec futures back toward the $4.00 area. For soybeans, continued Argentine
and Brazilian competition paired with ongoing Chinese absence could pressure Nov futures toward
$9.70–$9.80. A prolonged U.S. government shutdown after Oct 1 that halts USDA reports and
inspections could also sap confidence. Macro risks, such as a firmer U.S. dollar or weaker global equity
sentiment, would likewise weigh on futures.

Watchlist (Sep 25 – Oct 1):

Quarterly Stocks & Small Grains Summary (Sep 30): Final old-crop corn and soybean carry-in, plus
updated wheat production.

USDA Export Sales (Sep 25): Monitor for any Chinese demand in soybeans or additional corn flash
sales to Mexico.

Mississippi River logistics: Critically low levels continue to inflate freight and weaken Gulf bids; any
rainfall or Army Corps measures could improve flows.

South American weather: Brazil needs consistent rains by early October to accelerate soybean planting;
Argentina’s planting pace depends on both moisture and financing post-tax holiday.

Macro signals: Government shutdown risk, crude oil direction, and currency moves (USD, BRL, ARS) all
remain market shapers.

In summary: The grain complex enters late September with a neutral-to-bearish tilt. Supplies are ample
enough to weigh on flat prices, yet not burdensome enough to trigger distressed selling. Commercials are
content to carry grain at profitable spreads, while end-users benefit from weak export basis and firm
processor bids. The pivot from weather to harvest means that yield reports, stocks data, and export flows

Ethanol for America Act

The major barrier to a higher Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) is the fuel versus food debate.  Now is the
perfect time to move to E15.  Corn prices are extremely low, and the latest USDA WASDE reported a record
crop and a record crop expansion.

USDA WASDE forecast corn production at 16.7 billion bushels, with the yield at 188.8 bushels per acre and
ending  stocks  at  2.1  billion. The  season-average  corn  price  dropped  30  cents  to  $3.90  a  bushel.    Most
importantly, 202526 crop year is expected to have 1.8 billion more bushels than 2024/25.

With current ethanol production consuming 5.6 billion bushels, a 10 percent increase in consumption would
consume an additional 560 million bushels.  The reality is that year around E15 would add approximately
50 million bushels and regulatory streamlining would add another 50 million bushels.  At some point over
the  next  ten  years,  the  additional  corn  consumption  could  be  2.8  billion  bushels  minus  lower  fuel
consumption resulting from electric vehicles.  The ethanol corn consumption increase is likely to be more
of a S curve as new compacity comes online.

With  Brazil  and  the  U.S.  corn  and  soybean  production  increasing,  100  million  bushels  of  ethanol  corn
consumption will barely move the needle.  The ethanol production capacity expansion that would be three
to four years from now is when the corn consumption impact to the corn market would be felt.
