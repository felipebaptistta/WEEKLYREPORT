Corn – Exports Now Running Ahead of What the
Balance Sheet Requires
Corn firmed into early March, with May settling near $4.49,
up about six cents on the week, as shipments continue to
run ahead of seasonal requirements. Weekly inspections
moderated to 1.86 MMT but pushed cumulative shipments
to 39.6 MMT, now 45 percent ahead of last year, while
roughly 75 percent of USDA’s 83.83 MMT export forecast is
already sold. With the four-week shipment average near
1.54 MMT versus a required 0.80 MMT pace through
August, demand is beating what the balance sheet needs at
this point in the calendar. Even so, the March–May spread

holds near a 12-cent carry, still signaling comfortable nearby coverage. CIF Gulf barge basis eased to
+85H from +92H last week, ethanol bids remain firm, and managed money stays meaningfully net short,
supportive flow underneath a curve that still pays storage.

Wheat – Front-End Flips as Funds Cover into Persistent Black Sea Pressure
Chicago SRW’s front end tightened sharply, with the March–May spread flipping into roughly a 4-cent
inversion after carrying near 10 cents last week, even as May settled near $5.80 and held only marginal
gains week over week. Weekly inspections slowed to 0.34 MMT, yet cumulative shipments remain nearly
19 percent ahead of last year, and 93 percent of USDA’s export projection is already committed. Russian
12.5 percent FOB wheat near $234/MT continues to anchor global pricing at a meaningful discount to
U.S. Gulf equivalents, limiting sustained competitiveness. Managed money moved from a net short into a
modest net long position, suggesting short covering contributed to the structural tightening. While exports
remain steady, the inversion appears more positioning-driven than fundamentally restrictive, and absent a
shift in global supply or pricing spreads, the broader balance sheet remains heavy.

Soybeans – Structure Firms Slightly as Brazil Expands Its Window
Soybeans recovered, with May settling near $11.73, up roughly 15 cents on the week, though export flow
remains seasonally subdued as Brazil accelerates shipments. Weekly inspections improved to 1.14 MMT,
but cumulative shipments at 26.18 MMT remain 30 percent below last year, consistent with Brazil’s
expanding export program, with ANEC projecting March shipments near 15.7 MMT. The March–May
spread narrowed to roughly a 10-cent carry, about 46 percent of full carry, down from 70 percent last
week, suggesting stabilization in nearby structure rather than expanding storage incentives. CIF Gulf
barge basis firmed modestly to +90H, and interior processor bids remain steady, reflecting continued
domestic pull. Managed money remains net short, and while domestic demand continues to anchor the
complex, Brazil’s seasonal pricing ceiling remains firmly in place.

Macro / Policy – Ethanol Softens Slightly as Dollar Remains a Headwind

The U.S. dollar remained firm through the week, preserving a modest competitiveness headwind across
export channels. Crude oil held relatively stable in the low-to-mid $60s per barrel range, offering limited
directional influence on biofuel margins. Ethanol production for the week ending February 27 declined to
1.095 million barrels per day from 1.113 previously, while stocks increased to 26.34 million barrels from
25.40. The combination points to steady grind activity but limited margin expansion, particularly as cash
corn premiums remain firm across parts of the Midwest. Macro flows continue to play a secondary role
relative to physical demand signals, with grain markets trading primarily on export execution and balance
sheet mechanics rather than policy catalysts.

Weather – Argentina Stress Persists as U.S. Warms

NOAA’s 6–10 day outlook continues to favor above-normal temperatures across much of the central
United States, easing winter logistics friction and improving transportation conditions along major river
corridors. Brazil’s soybean harvest has reached roughly 45 percent completion, with safrinha planting
progressing near 47 percent despite localized rainfall interruptions across parts of the north. Argentina
remains the primary weather-sensitive variable, with soybean good-to-excellent ratings near 18 percent
and corn near 26 percent. While scattered showers have provided partial relief, roughly one-quarter of the
crop remains rated poor. Official production estimates remain unchanged near 47.5 MMT for soybeans
and 55.0 MMT for corn, but yield sensitivity remains elevated as crops advance through key reproductive
stages.

Corn Market Deep Dive
Futures & Spreads: Corn futures advanced modestly on the week, with May settling near $4.49 versus
roughly $4.43 last Wednesday, extending the recovery that began in late February. The more important
signal remains in the curve: the March–May spread holds near a 12-cent carry, roughly 80 percent of full
carry under the two-month storage framework. Carry widened slightly from last week’s 11 cents,
reinforcing that the market continues to reward storage and signal comfortable nearby coverage rather
than front-end stress, even as the board firms. Realized volatility remains moderate near 14 percent
annualized, consistent with a market grinding higher rather than breaking into a higher-beta trend, and
price action continues to reflect execution-driven support more than a structural tightening of supplies.

Funds & Positioning: Managed money remains net short in corn near -286,685 contracts as of the
February 24 report, but the week featured meaningful short covering with a +103,702 contract
improvement from the prior week. That shift matters because it confirms the rally is no longer only
commercial-led; some speculative pressure is being relieved as execution stays strong. At the same time,
open interest in May declined roughly 9,000 contracts from Feb 25 to Mar 3, suggesting the recent move
has leaned more toward liquidation than aggressive new length. In other words, positioning remains
defensive, but the market is starting to force risk reduction. Importantly, spreads have not collapsed
despite the large remaining net short, indicating the market is absorbing speculative flow without signaling

Cash & Basis: Cash markets cooled modestly after last week’s strength but still read as constructive. CIF
Gulf barge basis eased to roughly +85H from +92H, suggesting demand remains active but nearby offers

Export Sales & Inspections: Export execution remains the anchor. Weekly inspections eased from last
week’s surge but stayed strong at 1.86 MMT for the week ending Feb 26, lifting cumulative shipments to

average shipment pace near 1.54 MMT sits well above the roughly 0.80 MMT required weekly pace to
meet USDA’s projection through August. That gap suggests the balance sheet has room for additional
tightening if execution persists, even if weekly sales remain choppy.

Global Context: Global supply remains ample, but U.S. origin continues to hold competitive footing in
core channels. Black Sea offers remain steady, and Argentine FOB values are currently cheaper into
some destinations, but U.S. execution continues to be supported by dependable Western Hemisphere
pull, particularly Mexico. Brazil remains focused on soybean logistics and safrinha development, limiting
immediate corn export pressure in the near term. The global environment still caps runaway rallies, but
the U.S. program is performing well enough that competition has not yet forced a structural deterioration
in spreads or basis.

So What? Corn is firming while still trading like a carry market. The curve continues to reward storage,
but export execution is running above what the balance sheet requires, and speculative shorts are being
reduced rather than added. With May open interest drifting lower and managed money still meaningfully
net short, the market retains a defensive overlay, yet downside pressure remains contained by physical
flow and shipment momentum. Unless inspections materially cool or global competition sharpens, corn is
increasingly set up to trade as a stable carry market with tightening risk skewed toward the USDA export
line rather than toward a collapse in structure.

Soybean Market Deep Dive

Futures & Spreads: Soybean futures moved higher this week, with May settling near $11.73 compared
with roughly $11.58 last Wednesday. The more notable shift occurred in the structure of the curve: the
March–May spread tightened to roughly a 10 cent carry, equivalent to about 45 percent of full carry under
the two-month storage framework. Last week’s structure had widened toward 16 cents, so the
compression signals that the market is no longer expanding storage incentives in response to Brazilian
harvest pressure. Nearby coverage remains comfortable and the market is still clearly in contango, but
the tightening spread suggests that the board is beginning to absorb seasonal supply rather than discount
it further. Price action therefore reflects stabilization within a carry structure rather than a directional
breakout.

Products & Crush: Domestic processing remains the primary source of underlying demand support for
the soybean complex. Crushers remain well incentivized to operate at high utilization rates, though the
margin structure is increasingly concentrated on the meal side of the balance sheet rather than on oil.
Soybean oil inventories remain comfortable, limiting the contribution from the energy side of the complex,
while steady feed demand continues to support meal pricing. The result is a processing sector that is
maintaining throughput rather than accelerating it. In effect, crush demand is acting as a stabilizing force

February 26, an improvement from earlier weeks but still leaving cumulative shipments near 26.18 MMT,
roughly 30 percent below last year’s pace. Total export commitments stand around 35.65 MMT, or
approximately 83 percent of USDA’s 42.87 MMT export projection. The remaining shipment requirement
is manageable, and the recent pace suggests the United States can still meet the current forecast, but the
trade rotation clearly favors Brazil during this portion of the marketing year. Export demand therefore
remains functional, though it is no longer the primary driver of price formation.

Global Context: Brazil remains the defining force in the global soybean balance sheet. CONAB’s latest
estimate places production near 178 MMT, confirming another record crop as harvest progresses across
the central producing states. ANEC projects March soybean exports near 15.7 MMT, underscoring the
scale of Brazilian logistics as export flows accelerate. FOB Santos offers remain competitively priced into
Asia relative to U.S. Gulf values, reinforcing Brazil’s dominance during the peak shipping window.
Argentina’s crop outlook remains closer to 47–48 MMT, though crop ratings remain historically weak after

defines supply while Argentina represents the remaining weather variable.

moved higher on the week and the March–May spread tightened materially, signaling that the market is
beginning to absorb Brazilian harvest flow rather than expanding storage incentives. Domestic processing
continues to provide the structural demand floor, while export activity remains active but secondary during
South America’s peak shipping window. With managed money now positioned net short rather than long,
the market carries less liquidation risk but remains sensitive to shifts in global supply expectations. Unless
South American production expectations change materially or U.S. export competitiveness improves,
soybean price discovery is likely to remain anchored by steady domestic crush demand and the pace of
Brazilian export flow.

Wheat Market Deep Dive

Futures & Spreads: Chicago SRW wheat futures edged higher during the week, with May settling near
$5.80 compared with roughly $5.79 last Wednesday. The more meaningful development occurred in the
nearby structure: the March–May spread flipped from carry into a modest inversion near four cents. That
shift represents a notable change from the carry structure observed in recent weeks and indicates
tightening nearby availability rather than an expansion of storage incentives. Volatility also moved higher
relative to corn and soybeans, reflecting the market’s sensitivity to shifting trade flows and speculative
repositioning. While the flat price move itself was limited, the spread inversion signals that the front end of
the curve has become more responsive to short-term supply and logistics dynamics.

Cash & Basis: Cash markets showed a firmer tone across export corridors. CIF Gulf SRW values are
currently near +110H, indicating steady demand for nearby export slots despite limited improvement in
global competitiveness. Plains HRW basis remains defensive near 40 cents under May futures, reflecting
adequate regional coverage rather than aggressive commercial bidding. Pacific Northwest values were
largely unchanged, with export programs continuing to operate at routine levels. Overall basis behavior
suggests that the nearby inversion is being driven more by short-term positioning and logistics dynamics
than by a widespread tightening in underlying physical supply.

Export Sales & Inspections: U.S. wheat export activity slowed somewhat during the latest reporting
week. Export inspections for the week ending February 26 totaled roughly 0.34 MMT, below the prior
week’s pace but still leaving cumulative shipments near 18.62 MMT, roughly 19 percent ahead of last
year. Export commitments now stand near 93 percent of USDA’s marketing-year projection, meaning only
modest additional sales are required to reach the forecast before the May marketing-year close. While the
export program remains steady, shipment volumes alone are unlikely to materially tighten the U.S.
balance sheet without an improvement in global competitiveness.

Global Context: International wheat pricing continues to be shaped by Black Sea supply. Russian 12.5
percent FOB offers are currently near $234 per metric ton, maintaining a roughly $29 per ton discount to
comparable U.S. Gulf SRW values. That pricing advantage continues to anchor global trade flows into
traditional import markets across North Africa and the Middle East. European wheat values have firmed
modestly alongside the broader complex, but Black Sea origin remains the dominant pricing reference. As
long as Russian exports continue moving without disruption, the global wheat market remains structurally
well supplied despite short-term price fluctuations.

Funds & Positioning: Speculative positioning has shifted notably. Managed Money moved into a modest
net long position near 17,700 contracts in Chicago SRW wheat following several weeks of short covering.
The adjustment reflects the market’s sensitivity to spread tightening and volatility rather than a wholesale
shift in the global supply narrative. Even with the move into net long territory, speculative exposure
remains relatively small compared with prior cycles. The current positioning suggests that funds have
reduced downside conviction but have not yet established aggressive bullish exposure.

So What? Wheat’s tone has improved modestly, but the broader balance sheet remains heavy. The shift
of the March–May spread into inversion highlights tightening in the nearby structure and greater
sensitivity to short-term positioning and logistics flows. At the same time, export competitiveness remains
constrained by the persistent Black Sea pricing advantage, and global supply availability continues to limit
sustained upside. With speculative positioning shifting away from outright shorts and volatility elevated
relative to corn and soybeans, wheat may experience sharper short-term price swings. However, without
a meaningful disruption to global export flows or a production shock in a major origin, the market remains
fundamentally constrained by ample world supply.

US Cash Market & Basis

Corn: Corn basis held relatively firm across key export corridors even as futures edged higher during the
week. CIF Gulf barge values remained near +85H, reflecting consistent export loading and active river
movement. Interior river bids were roughly 20 cents under May futures, suggesting that elevators
continue to source grain without needing to aggressively lift bids. Ethanol plants also maintained
supportive bids as processors compete locally to secure supplies for steady grind rates. While basis has
cooled slightly from the stronger levels seen earlier in the winter, export shipments and ethanol demand
continue to provide the underlying support for the nearby cash market.

Soybeans: Soybean basis showed little directional change as global trade flows continue to rotate toward
South American origin. CIF Gulf barge bids are currently near +90H, indicating ongoing export movement
but below the stronger premiums observed earlier in the season. Interior processor bids around 40 cents
under May futures suggest crushers have sufficient nearby coverage while maintaining strong run rates.
With Brazilian exports ramping up rapidly during the harvest window, U.S. soybeans are moving into a
more secondary role in international trade, leaving the domestic cash market stable but lacking strong
upward pressure.

Wheat: Wheat cash markets leaned slightly firmer along export channels. CIF SRW basis near +110H
reflects consistent vessel demand at the Gulf despite the persistent price advantage held by Black Sea
suppliers. In the Plains, HRW basis remains near 40 cents under May futures, indicating comfortable
regional supply levels and routine commercial activity. Pacific Northwest values were largely unchanged
as export programs continue to operate at typical capacity. Basis strength at the Gulf therefore appears
linked more to shipping schedules than to tightening inventories across the broader wheat market.

So What? Cash market signals across the grain complex continue to point toward active commercial
movement rather than tightening availability. Corn basis remains comparatively supported by export flow
and ethanol demand, soybeans are steady but constrained by Brazil’s expanding export presence, and
wheat basis reflects normal export scheduling despite intense global competition. Overall, basis behavior
suggests that grain is moving through the system without difficulty, with demand channels operating
steadily but not forcing a repricing of nearby supply.

International Cash & Trade Flows

Brazil: Brazil’s soybean export pipeline is now shifting from harvest progress to shipment scale. ANEC
projects March exports near 15.7 MMT, indicating that port logistics are moving into the highest loading
period of the marketing year as freshly harvested supplies reach terminals. FOB Santos values around
$429/MT remain competitive relative to U.S. Gulf offers and continue to attract Asian demand during the
seasonal export rotation. Corn exports remain minimal for now, with ANEC projecting roughly 0.47 MMT
in March as exporters wait for the much larger safrinha crop later in the year. For the moment, Brazil’s
influence on global pricing is coming less from crop size and more from the speed at which soybeans are
entering the export pipeline.

Argentina: Argentina’s production outlook continues to carry uncertainty as crops move through late
development stages. Current estimates place soybean production near 47.5 MMT and corn near 55
MMT, though crop conditions remain uneven following earlier heat stress. Recent rainfall has improved
moisture levels in parts of the Pampas, but yield potential remains variable across regions. Export offers
are present but relatively limited compared with Brazil’s shipment volume, leaving Argentina’s role in
global markets tied more to potential supply revisions than to current trade flow.

China: Chinese import demand remains aligned with seasonal pricing incentives. Brazilian soybeans
continue to hold a cost advantage over U.S. Gulf offers, leading Chinese buyers to concentrate forward

Brazil’s soybean shipments are expanding rapidly as harvest moves through the system, while Argentina
remains the key production uncertainty that could influence later-season balances. In wheat, price

import markets. For now, global grain flows remain fluid and well supplied, with pricing differences rather

Ethanol Production & Stocks: Ethanol production eased slightly during the latest reporting week.
Output declined to 1.095 million barrels per day, down roughly 18,000 barrels per day from the previous

steady output alongside demand that remains stable but not accelerating. Margin proxies for Midwest

The ethanol sector continues to provide consistent demand for corn, though the build in inventories
suggests the industry is operating at a steady equilibrium rather than expanding grind rates further.

South American Production Updates: South American supply remains the dominant global variable.
Brazil’s CONAB raised its soybean production estimate to 177.98 MMT, confirming another record crop
as harvest advances across the main producing regions. Corn production was revised slightly lower to
138.44 MMT, reflecting localized adjustments in early crop performance ahead of the safrinha cycle. In
Argentina, soybean production estimates remain near 47.5 MMT, while corn production is projected
around 55 MMT, though crop condition ratings remain historically weak following periods of heat stress
earlier in the season. While Brazil’s record crop continues to expand global supply availability, Argentina
remains the key weather-sensitive variable capable of introducing volatility later in the season.

NOPA Soybean Crush: January U.S. soybean crush totaled 221.56 million bushels, beating
expectations near 218.5 million and confirming that processors continue to run at high utilization rates.
Strong soybean meal demand is sustaining crush margins even as soybean oil inventories remain
comfortable, limiting the contribution from the energy side of the complex. The result is a domestic
processing sector that continues to absorb U.S. soybeans steadily, providing balance sheet support while
global export demand shifts toward Brazil during the peak harvest shipping window.

CFTC Positioning (as of February 24, released February 28): Speculative positioning shifted notably
across the grain complex. Managed Money expanded its net short in corn to roughly 286,685 contracts,
reflecting continued skepticism toward sustained rallies despite strong export execution. In soybeans,
funds also moved into a deeper net short position near 171,392 contracts, reversing earlier constructive
positioning and highlighting the market’s adjustment to Brazil’s harvest-driven export window. Wheat
positioning moved in the opposite direction, with funds shifting to a modest net long near 17,758 contracts
after several weeks of short covering. The repositioning suggests that speculative flows are becoming
increasingly differentiated across the complex rather than uniformly defensive.

So What? Fundamental signals across the grain complex are becoming increasingly uneven. Corn
continues to lead on export execution, with shipment pace comfortably exceeding the level required to
meet USDA’s forecast. Soybeans remain supported by steady inspections but continue to operate under
the shadow of Brazil’s expanding export flow. Wheat fundamentals remain comparatively neutral, with
adequate export coverage but persistent global competition. Speculative positioning is also diverging
across crops, with funds heavily short corn and soybeans while shifting toward a modest long in wheat.
The result is a market structure where demand performance supports corn, seasonal supply caps
soybeans, and wheat remains primarily influenced by global pricing dynamics rather than domestic
fundamentals.

DEF Guidance Clarification

Starting  in the  early 2010s,  regulations required  all new  on-road diesel vehicles, tractors,  trucks,  buses,
ships,  locomotives,  gas  turbines  and  construction  equipment,  to  install  additional  technology  to  reduce
nitrogen oxide emissions, a byproduct of diesel. Almost all commercial diesel transportation and equipment
today has Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR) technology that runs on DEF.

According to an EPA release, "EPA issued clear guidance urging engine and equipment manufacturers to
revise DEF system software in existing vehicles and equipment to prevent these sudden shutdowns, giving
operators more time to repair faults without impacting productivity or safety."

The  EPA  provided  a  chart  showing  derate  timing  for  heavy  duty  trucks  will  be  extended  from  a  5-mph
limitation after just four hours' worth of a system fault to a three-stage derate:

  15 percent torque reduction at 650 miles / 10 hours
  30 percent torque reduction then at 4,200 miles / 80 hours
  and a final-stage derate to 25 mph at 8,400 miles / 160 hours

"In addition to providing certainty to manufacturers about how EPA wants this issue resolved, the agency
is  not  requiring  separate  approvals  beyond  that  provided  in  EPA’s  guidance,"  the  release  said.  "This
ensures that bureaucratic steps do not delay manufacturers’ ability to put solutions into the field.”

Before  the  new  EPA  DEF  guidance,  a  farmer  had  four  hours  before  the  vehicle  or  equipment  stopped
operating, leaving the farmer stranded.  EPA’s new guidance to engine manufacturers will reduce derating,
an automatic trigger forcing the engine to operate at a reduced capacity far below its capability and increase
the amount of time operators must secure DEF and refuel.

Previously, farmers and truckers faced rapid engine slowdown to 5 mph within just four hours of a sensor
alert. Under EPA’s new guidance for heavy-duty trucks, for example, after a fault is detected, only a warning
light appears for 650 miles or 10 hours. Most significantly, there is no performance impact for at least one
workday. Then, the engine only mildly derates by 15 percent, but this does not limit speed, allowing trucks
to operate normally for up to 4,200 miles or two work weeks. Only after about four work weeks does speed
drop to 25 mph until repairs are made. Most operators are expected to fix issues well before reaching this
limit. Moving forward for farmers, non-road equipment will not experience an impact for the first 36 hours.

The ruling represents a major step up from the status quo by giving operators significantly more time to
address faults before experiencing  severe engine  or speed limitations,  which will improve  their ability to
complete daily operations and reduce downtime.

The revised DEF guidance is estimated to reduce farming expenses by $727 million annually. With fewer
disruptions,  reduced  costs  for  emergency  towing,  repairs,  rentals,  DEF  deliveries  and  inventories,
producers will have greater operational and financial control.

Truck Manufacturers Sue CARB/California

Four of America’s top truck makers have officially filed a lawsuit in the US District Court for the Eastern
District of California challenging the Clean Truck Partnership (CTP) agreement signed with the California
Air Resources Board in 2023.  CARB has argued since the Congressional rollback of its Advanced Clean
Trucks  and  Omnibus  Low-NOx  rules  earlier  this  summer  that  despite  the  federal  disapproval  of  the
regulations, the CTP is still binding.

Cummins,  Daimler  Trucks  North  America,  Hino,  Isuzu,  then-Navistar,  Paccar,  the  Truck  and  Engine
Manufacturers Association, and Volvo Group North America  in July 2023 were  among 11 signers of the
CTP, an agreement with CARB that pledged truck and engine manufacturers will meet California’s vehicle
standards and required the sale and adoption of zero-emission vehicle (ZEV) technology.

The  OEMs said in the lawsuit that despite the Congressional Review  Acts  passed  this  year preempting
California’s emissions standards, the state “continues to demand compliance with its heavy-duty emissions
standards, and it has threatened OEMs that design, develop, manufacture and sell heavy-duty vehicles and
engines ... with civil sanctions and unfavorable regulatory treatment if the OEMs refuse to comply with the
state’s  unlawful  standards,  while  also  taking  measures  to  insulate  itself  from  lawful  challenges  to  those
standards.”

Senior  Clean  Vehicles  Advocate  for  the  Natural  Resources  Defense  Council  Guillermo  Ortiz  called  the
lawsuit, "a cynical reversal of course. These companies helped negotiate the Clean Truck Partnership to
secure regulatory certainty. Now they’re trying to dismantle the very deal they shaped -- injecting instability
into a market they claim to lead," he said. "This is bad faith, plain and simple, and it raises questions about
whether these manufacturers are serious about their ability to deliver clean trucks to the global stage."

The OEMs said the US Department of Justice has sent them Cease & Desist letters stating that California’s
enforcement  of its regulations “is contrary to  federal  law” and  directed  the  truck  makers “to  immediately
cease and desist compliance with California’s preempted and unlawful mandates.”

This  has  left  the  OEMs  “caught  in  the  crossfire,”  they  said,  as  “California  demands  that  OEMs  follow
preempted laws; the United States  maintains such laws are illegal and  orders OEMs to  disregard them.
This situation is not tenable.”

As such, the lawsuit aims to clarify the OEMs’ legal obligations under federal and state law and to block
California from enforcing regulations that are preempted by federal law.  Given the inconsistencies between
federal  and  California  regulatory  requirements  for  emissions,  truck  and  engine  makers  and  their  trade
associations in 2023 petitioned CARB for relief from the state’s “infeasible regulatory requirements,” which
resulted in the CTP, the lawsuit added.  Now, according to the OEMs, “California seeks to use the Clean
Truck Partnership, which was intended to harmonize compliance with state and federal law, to compel an
entire industry to follow California emissions standards that are now in conflict with federal law, and which
have been expressly preempted by federal law.”  They added that the state is disregarding the CTP’s “plain
language,”  which  “states  that  manufacturers  are  obligated  to  follow  CARB’s  emissions  regulations
‘regardless of the outcome of any litigation challenging … those regulations, or CARB’s overall authority to
implement those regulations,’ not federal legislative action taken to invalidate them.”

The regulatory back-and-forth between the federal government and California/CARB has put OEMs “in an
impossible position,” they said in the lawsuit. “As a result, the OEMs are subject to two sovereigns whose
regulatory  requirements  are  irreconcilable  and  who  are  openly  hostile  to  one  another.  Each  wields  a
hammer  to  enforce  its  will  on  industry,  leaving  OEMs  --  who  simply  seek  to  sell  heavy-duty  trucks  in

to be certified for sale and by which regulatory authority.”

manufacturing supply managers reported switching from international suppliers to domestic suppliers. More
